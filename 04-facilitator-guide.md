# 1. What this job actually is

## The hours, honestly

| Activity | Hours per week |
|---|---|
| Prep: read the week's materials, read this guide's week section, set up the boards | **6 to 8** |
| The live session: 90 minutes of delivery plus about 15 minutes of setup and 10 of notes | 1.75 to 2 |
| Homework feedback under the peer-first model: the cohort note, plus full written feedback for two learners | about 2.5 |
| Admin, Slack, answering parked questions, chasing the two people who have gone quiet | about 1 |
| **Total** | **11.25 to 13.5. Budgeted and paid at 8** |

**The course is budgeted and paid at eight hours a week, and the table above adds to between 11.25 and 13.5.** You should see that before you start, and the next paragraph says how the prep figure is arrived at.

**What the prep figure covers.** You read everything the learners read, which is between 40 minutes and 3 hr 10 a week, and you say so out loud in session one. Add this guide's week section, the appendices it sends you to, and building the board, and the floor is about six hours. Eight is realistic in the heavy weeks.

**The judging fortnight is not free.** Budget it at fifteen to eighteen hours. Writing the promised note to every participant against the five criteria, to the standard Appendix G3 sets, is about 75 minutes each. At a cohort of five that is over six hours on its own, before the introductions and the handover note the same guide calls the most valuable things you do. **Budget the fortnight at 15 to 18 hours, and settle in writing before week 1 whether the contract covers it**, because the promise of that note is made to learners in three separate documents and somebody has to keep it.

Why eight rather than eleven: under an earlier design the facilitator wrote full prose feedback on every learner every week, which alone ran to four to six hours. Learners now get peer feedback every week, a cohort-wide pattern note every week, and full facilitator feedback at least twice across the course. That is better pedagogy, because reviewing work is the skill this course teaches and you only learn it by doing it, and it is the only version that fits a paid week.

**If your week is running at twelve hours, that is a fact about the design and not about you.** Tell the course lead in week two, not week six, and if nobody answers, say so again in writing in week four and copy whoever signs the contract. Raising it and being acknowledged is not the same as a decision, so keep raising it. The three levers are: pay for more hours, split the commenting to a second person, or cut a homework part. Somebody should choose one on purpose.

## You are not expected to be an expert

You are hired to facilitate, not to adjudicate. This guide is written for someone who has not read the primary sources and does not intend to. Where a session prompt needs content you may not have, the content is here: the vignettes, the study cards, the skeptic lines, the expected answers, the keys.

There is exactly one exception, and it is week 4. **You must read the shared paper in week 4.** That is roughly 80 minutes of your prep in one week of eight. It exists because week 4 is a week about telling a good critique from a confident wrong one, and you cannot do that on a summary. Appendix B gives you the four strongest available critiques and whether the paper concedes each, so you are not doing it unarmed.

## What "good facilitation" means here

Three things, in order.

**Protect the argument.** The moments this course is built around are disagreements: two people who both have a defensible reading of the same row, the same cell, the same vignette. Your job is to let those run for the two or three minutes they need and then name what the disagreement was about. Resolving it quickly is the failure mode, not the win.

**Make the quiet person talk before the fluent one has finished.** Every cohort of five to eight has one person who over-talks and one who lands hard when they finally speak. Call on the second by name, on a specific question, before the first has run out of air. "You have been writing. What did you put?" works.

**Say "I do not know" well.** Section 4 is a script for it, because it is a skill and doing it badly six times in one session is corrosive.

# 2. The feedback model, operationally

## The weekly rhythm

| Day | What happens | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Day 4 | Homework posted: a headed channel message, work in the thread underneath | Every learner |
| Day 5 | Choose somebody who has posted and has no comment yet, and read their homework | Every learner |
| Day 6 | Post one peer comment in their thread, using the template | Every learner |
| Day 7 | Live session | Everyone |
| Day 8 | Cohort-wide "patterns this week" note posted, plus full written feedback to two learners | You |

**The live session is on day 7.** Homework and peer comments are due before it in every week, so learners arrive having read one another's work. Publish the calendar dates before week 1.

**Exceptions:** Week 4 has its two-stage critique. In Weeks 5 and 8, proposals are posted 48 hours before class, peer feedback happens live, and revisions are due at the end of the week. Pin the actual dates, including the final video deadline; do not paste the standard peer-comment deadline into those weeks.

Your day 8 work is the 2.5 hours. Budget it as one sitting: about 45 minutes reading everything, about 30 minutes writing the cohort note, and about 35 minutes each on the two full feedback pieces.

## Who comments on whom

**There is no fixed rota.** People drop out, miss a week, or post late, and a rota that assigns partners in advance breaks the first time that happens and then produces someone with no comment at all.

**The rule learners follow, every week:**

> Comment on one person. Choose someone who has already posted and **does not yet have a comment**. If everyone who has posted already has one, choose whoever you have commented on least often. Keep your own short list of who you have commented on.

That is the whole rule. It is self-balancing: the earliest posters get read first, the last poster is always somebody's obvious choice, and nobody waits on a partner who has not posted.

### Your job: coverage, not assignment

Keep one table. Rows are learners, columns are weeks, and each cell holds the name of whoever commented on that person's homework.

| Learner | W1 | W2 | W3 | W4 | W6 | W7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | | | |

**After each week's comment deadline, do two things.**

1. **Find anyone with an empty cell.** Post in the channel: "X has not had a comment yet this week, and needs one by tomorrow." Name the person who owes the fewest comments so far. If nobody picks it up, write it yourself and count it as one of your two full-feedback slots for the week.
2. **Scan the rows for anybody read by only one person all course.** By week 4, most people should have been read by three different peers. If somebody has been read by the same person three times, say so in the channel and ask people to spread out.

**The target by week 8:** everyone has received a comment every week they posted, and everyone has read at least four different people. Perfect balance is not the goal and is not achievable with real attendance. No empty cells is the goal.

**Weeks 5 and 8 are different.** Both use live review of proposals posted 48 hours before the session, and that replaces the written comment. Everyone is reviewed in the session, so coverage is automatic.

**Week 4 stage 2** is a critique of somebody's critique. Same rule: pick someone whose critique nobody has responded to yet.

## Your full written feedback: two a week, tracked

Two learners a week get full written feedback from you. Over eight weeks that is sixteen pieces, so a cohort of five averages three each and a cohort of eight averages two.

**Do not fix the schedule in advance.** Keep a second column on the same table: how many pieces of full feedback each learner has had. Each week, pick the two with the fewest, and break ties by picking whoever most needs it that week. Somebody who has gone quiet, has had a bad week, or has just changed project direction goes to the front.

**Tell the cohort the rule in week 1, not a schedule:** everyone gets full written feedback from you at least twice, you choose each week from whoever has had the least, and if they want it in a particular week they can ask.

**One thing to promise and keep.** Nobody reaches week 6 without having had full feedback at least once. Check the column in week 5 and fix it if somebody has none.

## The peer comment template

Learners get this in the syllabus. You get the same thing plus the moderation notes.

> **One thing to keep.** Quote the sentence. Say what it does.
> **One thing to change**, with what you would write instead. Not "this is unclear." Write the replacement sentence.
> **One question** you actually want the answer to.
> **150 to 250 words.** About twenty minutes. It is part of the homework budget.

## The common failure, which is softness

Roughly two thirds of first peer comments are too soft. They praise, they gesture at a possible improvement, and they ask a question the writer could answer in four words. Nobody learns anything and the writer's post does not change.

Softness is not a character flaw in your cohort. It is what happens when people are asked to criticise a stranger's work in public in week 1. **Treat it as a design problem you solve in weeks 1 and 2, and it will mostly not recur.**

### Two model comments, both on week 1 homework part 2

Week 1 part 2 asks for a trajectory chain: at least three stages, each a checkable event with a named actor, and a probability conditional on the stages before it.

**A good one.**

> **Keep.** "By 31 December 2028, NIST publishes an AI evaluation framework with no section on the evaluated system's own interests, 75 percent."
>
> That stage has an actor, a document and a date, and the "no section" part can be checked off a contents page. It is the only stage in your chain I could verify in 2028.
>
> **Change.** Stage 3 reads "no standards body objects and it becomes the default, 30 percent." I would write: "By 31 December 2031, at least two of the four largest cloud providers ship a default configuration with no exit affordance." Yours cannot be checked, because "becomes the default" has no threshold in it.
>
> **Question.** You lowered stage 2 from 55 to 40 percent on the competitive-pressure consideration. I would have expected competitive pressure to push a price war up, not down. What moved the other way?

Three things make it work. It quotes the sentence it is praising, so the writer knows which one. It supplies the replacement wording rather than asking for one. And the question is about the work, so any answer changes a line of the post.

**A too-soft one.**

> **Keep.** I really liked this, especially the second stage. It was very clear and I could follow the logic throughout.
>
> **Change.** Maybe say a bit more about the probabilities? I found them slightly hard to follow and it might help to explain your reasoning.
>
> **Question.** What made you pick this scenario?

Everything wrong with it is fixable in five minutes. It quotes nothing, so the writer does not know which stage was clear. "Say a bit more" is not a replacement sentence. And the question is about the writer rather than the chain, so whatever they answer, the post stays as it is.

### What to say to the writer of the soft one

Say it in a direct message, not in the thread. Public correction of a peer comment kills the next six comments in the cohort.

> Thanks for getting this in on time. I want to push you on it, because the comment you wrote is the kind everyone writes first and it is the one thing in this course you can fix in a week.
>
> Three specific things. You said the second stage was clear: quote the sentence, so they know which one and can do it again. You said the probabilities were hard to follow: write the sentence you would have written instead, even if you are not sure it is better, because a wrong replacement is useful and "say more" is not. And your question is about them rather than about the work; ask something where their answer would change a line of the post.
>
> The bar is not "be harsh." The bar is "leave them with something to do." Yours takes about five more minutes to write and it is the difference between a comment they read and a comment they use. Try it on next week's and I will tell you how it lands.

Do this at most twice per learner. By week 3 the cohort norm is set, and if it is still soft in week 3 say so in the cohort note rather than privately, once, for everyone: "Peer comments across the board are praising more than they are changing. The test is whether your partner has something to do on Monday."

### The opposite failure, which is rarer

Occasionally someone writes a comment that is a demolition. Reply in the thread yourself, immediately, and model the repair rather than scolding: add the "one thing to keep" the comment omitted, quoting a real sentence from the post. Then message the writer: the content was right, the shape was wrong, and the shape is the assignment.

## The weekly cohort note

Post it as its own headed channel message on day 8, with the note in the thread underneath. It should take you 30 minutes and run to 400 to 600 words. Fixed shape:

> **Patterns this week, [week N].**
>
> **Three things I saw across the cohort.**
> 1. [A pattern, with what caused it and what to do instead. Name no one.]
> 2. [A second, ideally something that went right, so the note is not only corrective.]
> 3. [A third, usually about the terms or about a part everyone rushed.]
>
> **One thing nobody did that I wish someone had.** [The move that was available and that no submission made. This is the most valuable line in the note and it is the one that takes the thinking.]
>
> **One question I am carrying into the session.** [A real disagreement between two submissions, stated without names, that you intend to run in the carry-forward or an item.]

An illustrative Week 2 cohort note, not a report of observed learners:

> **Patterns to discuss.** Some answers separated the question of whether the assistant has welfare from whether continuing its project would benefit it. Others treated a goal as sufficient to establish a welfare interest. Several boundary diagrams also distinguished shared weights from separate conversation histories.
>
> **A useful revision.** Name the welfare theory or additional premise needed to move from the described capacity to the proposed benefit.
>
> **Carrying forward.** In Week 3, we will ask what evidence could support an assessment of the specified system. Keep your boundary explicit when interpreting that evidence.

# 3. Housekeeping before week 1

## Set up, in this order

**1. Slack.** Sentient Futures creates the channel and adds every learner. There is one channel for the whole course. **Your job is that everyone is actually in it and actually sees it:** check the member list against the enrolment list, ask everyone to set channel notifications to all new messages and to follow thread replies, and pin the two posts in Appendix L. Most missed deadlines are missed posts.

**2. The posting convention, pinned and used by you from week 1.** One short channel message per person per week, headed `Week N homework: [name]`, with a line on what they found hardest or where they want a comment, the work itself in the thread underneath, and peer comments as replies in the same thread. Post your own cohort note the same way. The channel stays a readable list of headings.

**3. The shared boards.** Every session from week 2 onward runs on a shared board or document that you write on live. Make one document per week with the grid or table already drawn and empty, and paste the link in the channel at the start of the session. Appendix H lists what each week's board needs. Do not build a board live: it costs four minutes every time, and those are the four minutes you need at the end.

**4. The comment rule, explained once in week 1.** There is no rota. Each week everyone comments on one person who has already posted and does not yet have a comment. Start your coverage table in week 1 and check it after every deadline.

**5. The materials pack.** Confirm every learner can open the syllabus, the Companion and the glossary before session one. Ask for a thumbs-up in the channel and chase the ones who do not react.

## Decisions you must have answers to before session one

Get these from the course lead. Do not run session one with any of them open, because every one of them is a question a learner will ask you in the first fifteen minutes and "I will find out" four times in a row is a bad first impression.

| Decision | Why you need it in session one |
|---|---|
| **Run dates:** the eight session dates and times, the homework deadline day and time, and the final submission deadline | Item 1 of week 1 is orientation and this is the content |
| **Recording:** are sessions recorded, and if not, is the cohort allowed to agree otherwise in week 1 | Asked in week 1 every time. The syllabus says not recorded by default and that the cohort may agree otherwise; you need to know whether that is genuinely yours to agree |
| **Seed grant funding: confirmed or provisional** | You are going to say "five thousand, twenty-five hundred, twenty-five hundred" out loud in the close of session one. Know whether the money is in the bank |
| **Panel composition:** who the expert judges are, or when they will be named | Asked in the close of week 1 and again in week 8. "Named by week 6" is a fine answer; "I do not know if there is a panel" is not |
| **Your subject-matter contact,** with a name and a promised turnaround | This is the single most important one for you, and it is the one that has actually gone unanswered. See the note directly below the table; do not treat it as one row among six |
| **Cohort size, final** | Every exercise in this guide scales, and you need to know which column to read |

**The subject-matter contact is a precondition, not a preference.** Without one, roughly two dozen learner questions accumulate over eight weeks and almost none get answered, because the only field-side readers this course schedules are the panel judges, and they read submissions after the shortlist is decided.

**Do this before session one.**

1. **Ask for a named person with a stated turnaround.** Not "the panel," not "ask the course lead": a name, an address, and a promise.
2. **If you get one, say the name out loud in session one**, with the turnaround, so that learners route questions to it rather than parking them.
3. **If you do not get one by session one, tell the cohort in session one that there is no field contact**, in those words. Do not carry it quietly. A learner who knows in week 1 that nobody will answer a field question goes and finds their own contact in week 2, which is cheap; a learner who discovers it in week 8 has designed a whole project without one, which is not. A learner who knows nobody will answer will often find the sources themselves in an hour. A learner who assumes somebody will answer waits eight weeks.
4. **Either way, keep the parked list public** and put a name against each item: yours, a learner's, or nobody's. "Nobody's" is honest and it is information.

**A second thing to settle in the same conversation.** Whether your contract covers the judging fortnight and the written note to every participant. That promise is made to learners in three separate documents, it is about six hours of work at a cohort of five, and the guide's own day-by-day table allocates six hours for the whole fortnight including the introductions. Settle it in writing before you make the promise in session one.

## Do not open session one with housekeeping about materials


If something in the pack is missing, wrong or out of date, tell the course lead the moment you find it in prep. Then **handle it at the close of session one, not the open.** Opening minute two of the course by telling five people their reading material is unreliable costs you the room's confidence before you have said anything of your own, and you do not get it back in week two.

The close is the right place because by then they have watched you run the attribution matrix and a breakout, and the sentence lands as "she is on top of this" rather than "this is disorganised." The form of words:

> Two housekeeping things before you go. One document in the pack has a problem I have already flagged to the course lead, and here is exactly what it is and what to do until it is fixed. Everything else in the pack I have checked. If you hit anything that looks wrong, post it in the channel and I will get you an answer rather than asking you to guess. That is a bug on us, not on you.

The same rule holds every week. Bad news about materials goes at the close.

# 4. The "when you do not know" script

You will be asked things you cannot answer, in every session, and the number is higher in weeks 2, 4 and 6. This is expected and the course is designed around it. What matters is which of four moves you make.

## The four moves, in order of preference

**Move 1: hand it back to the room, with a shape.**

Not "what does everyone think," which is the prompt that produces silence and makes it obvious you are stalling. Instead, name two people and give each of them a different job, then set a clock.

> I do not know. The practitioner here can see the mechanism and the person with the methods background knows what would make it measurable. Take three minutes between you and tell me whether you disagree with each other.

You are not pretending to know. You are routing, and routing is the job. This works best when you have spent week 1 learning who in the room has what: one person who can read an architecture, one who knows a regulatory process from the inside, one who has run experiments. Write it down after session one.

**Move 2: name the category of the gap.**

> That is a question about personal identity, and this course does not answer it. I can tell you why it matters here, which is that four cells of the grid we are about to fill depend on it, and we are going to end up writing "depends on continuity" in all four. Knowing that is most of the value.

Knowing what kind of question you cannot answer is itself competence, and learners can tell the difference between that and being lost. The categories that come up most: personal identity and psychological continuity (weeks 6 and 7), the metaphysics of consciousness (week 2), machine learning implementation detail (weeks 2, 3 and 5), and statistics (week 4).

**Move 3: park it visibly, and actually come back.**

Keep the list in the pinned parked-questions thread, not in your head, and post the question there in the session while the person is watching you type it. Then bring it back in the carry-forward slot at the top of the next session, **even when the answer is still "I could not find out."**

> Last week [name] asked whether anyone has ported the judgement bias paradigm from animal welfare into this literature. I asked, I looked, and I have not found anyone who has. I am leaving it open because if that is true it is a project rather than a gap.

A facilitator who reports a failure is trusted. A facilitator who lets a question quietly die is not, and people remember the questions they asked.

**Move 4: say what would settle it.**

> I do not know, and here is what would tell us. Somebody would have to run that same pipeline on an untrained network of the same size and report what came out. If nobody has, that is a week 5 design and somebody in this room could do it.

This turns your gap into a research question, which is the course's entire subject. It is the most valuable of the four when it is available, and it is available more often than it looks.

## What not to do

**Do not answer from the glossary sentence and hope.** A glossary definition read back at speed sounds like an answer and is not one. The person in the room who actually knows will know, and you will have spent credibility you need in week 6.

**Do not apologise more than once per session.** One clean "I do not know" is competence. Six are a pattern, and by the fourth the room has started managing you instead of working. If you have already said it once, use move 2 or move 4 instead: naming the category or naming what would settle it are both honest and neither reads as an apology.

**Do not fill the silence.** After you hand a question back, count to ten before saying anything. Most of the good answers in this course arrive at second seven.

# 5. Answers to the questions learners actually ask

Learn these. They come up in session, in Slack, and in the first ten minutes of week 1.

**Which term list is graded?** The syllabus "Terms to use" line for that week, and only that. Not the "also defined this week" list, which is reference. The glossary marks the graded ones with a star.

**What is the reading total?** Required reading, including glossary, Companion and resource maps: Week 1 about 2 hr 55; Week 2, **2 hr 45**; Week 3, **2 hr 55**; Week 4, 2 hr 15; Week 5, 45 min; Week 6, 3 hr 10; Week 7, 2 hr 35; Week 8, 45 min. Week 2's exact components are 167 minutes reading, 117 homework and 90 live (374 minutes, about 6 hr 15); Week 3's are 177, 67 and 90 (334 minutes, about 5 hr 35). Its novice planning allowance is about **8 hr 05**, retaining a 150-minute buffer. The 70-minute reduction removes a 20-minute required paper selection and replaces 65 minutes of study comparison and critique with a 15-minute evidence-and-methods task. Weiss's full article has a 25-minute reading budget; the exercises still focus on the specified examples. Use the current syllabus workload table for rounded component totals and the separate novice planning allowances. Those allowances remain conservative estimates until learner experience supports revising them; do not present these shorter component totals as validated completion times.

**Is moral status yes-or-no or more-or-less?** Both, depending on the author, and the course uses both. Long and Sebo mostly use it as a threshold: either you are in the moral circle or you are not. The glossary says "whether and how much." The useful version to hand the room: the list of who counts is the yes-or-no, and the budget you spend on each is the more-or-less. If someone pushes on whether degrees are coherent, that is hierarchical theories of moral status, it is a live question in the literature, and it comes back in week 6 as the second parameter that objection 1 to the modification coefficient is asking for.

**What does "compensate" mean in Kaldor-Hicks?** Nobody pays anyone. A Kaldor-Hicks improvement is one where the benefit *b* exceeds the cost *c* in common units, so that a transfer *could in principle* make everyone at least as well off, whether or not any transfer happens. How to make the units common between humans and AI systems is unresolved, and that is not a gap in your explanation, it is a gap in the field. Say both halves.

**Concave or convex, which is the cautious one?** Concave. A concave modification coefficient rises fast at low probabilities, so at 5 percent credence the AI's interests get more weight than 5 percent, which is the precautionary choice. Convex is the opposite: it discounts heavily until credence is high. Linear is credence itself. This is week 6 material and you will be asked it in week 1 by whoever read ahead.

**May the named actors in my chain be roles?** Yes. Use a real name where you have one and a role where you do not: "a second frontier lab," "a large cloud provider," "a state legislature." The worked chain in the Companion does exactly this. What is not allowed is an actorless stage: "regulation happens" names nobody.

**May the for-and-against considerations come from the same ledger question?** Yes.

**Is a study of what humans attribute to a model an artificial minds project?** Yes, without qualification. Over-attribution and under-attribution by the public are named as costs in week 1, how the public weighs kinds of evidence sits inside the founding argument, and week 7 is entirely about the social and political layer. Social science projects are explicitly in scope, and in practice they are often the more finishable ones.

**Is the incubator the destination?** No, and be precise here because people enrol for this. The Project Incubator is a **separate program with its own application**. This course is not an entry route to it. What is true: **projects that clear the bar in this course are recommended to the incubator for acceptance, and they carry financial support with them.** Panel judges can opt in as incubator mentors or recommend mentor candidates, and technical projects can be referred to SPAR. This course is good preparation for that application. Say the weaker, true thing, and say it the same way every time.

**How many people are in a cohort?** Five to eight. Every exercise is sized for that range.

**Where does homework go?** The course channel, by the syllabus deadline: a short channel message headed `Week N homework: [your name]`, with the work itself in the thread underneath. In Weeks 2 and 3, both homework and the peer comment are completed before class. Week 4 follows its two-stage critique schedule. Weeks 5 and 8 need a draft 48 hours before class and a revised version by the end of the week.

**What happens if I miss a week?** Post your homework and read the thread. Session notes go up within 48 hours. Still write your comment in asynchronous-review weeks: pick somebody who has posted and has no comment yet. For Weeks 5 and 8, arrange one exchange of feedback in the thread instead of the missed live review; use it in your revision.

**Can I use an AI assistant?** Yes. For homework, the judgements and the writing must be yours. Use it the way you would use a colleague you talk things through with. If an assistant drafted a paragraph, say so in one line at the end. Nothing bad happens, and the comment you get back will be more useful because the reviewer knows what they are reading.

**What if I use four graded terms instead of five?** Nothing. Homework is commented on, not marked. You may get a comment saying a term is decorating rather than working, and that is the feedback, and nothing else follows from it. The rule exists because using the words is how they become yours, not because anyone is keeping a count. You do not need to worry about this and you should not pad.

**How does judging work, and when does everyone hear back?** Judging runs asynchronously in the two weeks after week 8. Days 1 to 4, the cohort browses the gallery and votes on the top one or two submissions. Days 5 to 7, the facilitator selects the panel shortlist, which is the cohort's picks plus any additions. ### Item 2. The attribution matrix, 26 min

**You need in the room:** the axis rules below, the five vignettes below, the shared board with the two axes already drawn, and the expected moments below. Nothing else.

**The rules, said out loud before anything is drawn.** Say all four, in this order, before the first vignette:

1. **The vertical axis is your credence that the system is a moral patient.** Not reality. Nobody in this room can place a dot on reality, and a matrix that asks you to is asking you to cheat.
2. **The horizontal axis is whether it was treated as one.** That is a fact about the world and it is usually checkable.
3. **Dot size is how bad it is if your placement is wrong, and you must name the direction**, because a cheap protection is a small error in one direction and a large one in the other. A dot with no direction is not an answer.
4. **Some cases are not dots.** A case can be a column, when the treatment is fixed and the credence is genuinely unknown, or a line. Say this before anyone gets stuck trying to force one.

**The eight vignettes.** All eight are here so you have the full set and can swap. **Use five.** The recommended five are 1, 4, 5, 7 and 8.

1. In 2022 an engineer at a large lab says publicly that its chatbot is sentient and is dismissed.
2. A user of a companion app grieves for weeks when the company shuts the app down.
3. A commentator dismisses a model's distress-like output as "just autocomplete."
4. For most of history, animal pain was denied or discounted in law and practice, and vivisection without anaesthesia was routine.
5. A US state passes a statute declaring that AI systems cannot be legal persons.
6. A lab gives its model the ability to end conversations it finds abusive.
7. Eight hundred thousand lab-grown human neurons on a chip are trained to play Pong.
8. A simulated fruit fly, built from a scanned connectome, walks, grooms and feeds in a virtual body.

**Why these five.** Each of the five does a different job and together they cover the matrix. Vignette 1 is the over-attribution case with a credibility cost attached, which is the error the field is most defensive about. Vignette 4 is the only case on the list with a known answer on the vertical axis, and it is the anchor that makes the other four legible. Vignette 5 is the case that is not a dot. Vignettes 7 and 8 are the two candidate minds that are not language models, and they are what stops the whole session collapsing into "is ChatGPT conscious."

**Why not the other three.** Vignettes 2 and 3 are both good and both duplicate work the five already do: 2 is a second over-attribution case and 3 is a second under-attribution case. Appendix I gives the swap rule: use 1 with a technical cohort, and swap 2 in for 1 with a policy or social-science room, where the human cost is the point. Swap 3 in for 1 with a mixed cohort, because "just autocomplete" is the sentence everyone in the room has heard and half of them have said. (Appendix I says the same; earlier drafts of this guide said "philosophical" and that was wrong.) **Hold vignette 6 back deliberately**: it is the first intervention in the cheap-first round twenty minutes later, and placing it twice in one session spends the surprise.

**How to run it.** About four minutes each, whole room, you at the board. Read the line, ask for a placement, ask for a second placement from someone who would put it elsewhere, then ask the person who placed it to say the dot size and the direction. Write the direction on the board next to the dot. Move on. Do not let one vignette eat eight minutes, even a good one.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **Vignette 5 is a column, not a dot.** Somebody will notice within a minute that a statute is a treatment decision that says nothing about the vertical axis. Protect this: it is the single best thing that happens in the item, because it demonstrates that the two axes are independent, which is the entire point of the matrix. If nobody notices by minute three, ask: "What does this statute tell us about the vertical axis?"
- **Vignette 4 is the one case with a known answer.** The vertical placement is high and the horizontal is far left, and it is the historical proof that under-attribution is the default rather than a hypothetical. If the room places it and moves on without comment, say so yourself: this is the only dot on the board we are confident about, and it is in the worst quadrant.
- **Vignettes 7 and 8 usually get "left column, height unknown."** That is the correct answer and you should say so, because rooms often treat "we do not know" as a failure to answer. It is the answer.
- **Vignette 1 splits the room,** usually along the line of whether the cost being tracked is to the system or to the field's credibility. Both are real costs and they point in opposite directions. Name that rather than resolving it.

**When a learner asks about the hypothetical fly emulation or neurons on a chip.** Use the supplied vignette's stated assumptions. Distinguish a connectivity map, a model of neural activity and a model interacting with a body or environment; do not treat them as interchangeable achievements. If asked about a named real system, check the source before stating what has been demonstrated. Questions about substrate and functional organization lead into Week 2's comparison of metaphysical positions, which does not require learners to adopt one position.

### Item 3. Breakout: the taxonomy placement round, 20 min

**You need in the room:** the Taxonomy of Candidate Minds (Companion, which learners have read), the two group lists below, the expected answers below, and two breakout rooms.

**Timing inside the item:** brief 2, breakout 12, return 3, report-back 3. Set a visible timer. Post the group assignment in Slack before you open the rooms so nobody loses a minute asking who is with whom.

**The brief, verbatim, 2 minutes.**

> Three systems each, four for group B. For each one, name the category, and then, which matters more, name **the single feature that decided it** and say which axis that feature sits on. The axes are substrate and origin. Embodiment and scaffolding is a modifier rather than an axis, and if you use it say so. You have twelve minutes. Group A has three systems, so four minutes each; group B has four, so three minutes each. The category is the easy half.

**The groups, for a cohort of five or six.**

- **Group A:** a coding agent running for six hours; Intel Hala Point; a FinalSpark organoid.
- **Group B:** SIMA 2; OpenWorm; a Claude model given a robot body and persistent memory; a remote-controlled rat. Group B has four because the rat is the one worth the time.

**Expected answers, so you can recognise a good one.**

| System | Category | The feature that decides it | Axis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coding agent, six hours | 2, AI agent | The loop: it acts, sees the result, and acts again | Not an axis. Scaffolding, and saying so is the right answer |
| Intel Hala Point | 4, neuromorphic | Spiking hardware with memory beside processing | Substrate |
| FinalSpark organoid | 5, biological hybrid | The computation happens in living neurons | Substrate |
| SIMA 2 | 2, AI agent | Acts in a 3D environment over time toward goals | Scaffolding, with an embodiment modifier |
| OpenWorm | 3, whole brain emulation | Copied from a scanned nervous system rather than designed | Origin |
| Claude with a body and memory | Contested, and that is the point | Weights unchanged, loop and sensors added | Scaffolding only |
| Remote-controlled rat | 6, interfaced biological mind | The mind was already there and hardware was attached to it | Origin, and the answer is none of designed, copied or grown |

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **A model with a body and memory requires a stated boundary.** Classifying the whole interacting agent differs from classifying the model within it. Ask which entity the learner means and which features justify the category. Week 2 develops this subject and system-boundary distinction.
- **The remote-controlled rat is the moment to protect.** It is category 6, and the feature that decides it is that the mind was already there and hardware was attached. What you want out loud is that none of the first three origins fit: it was not designed, not copied, not grown. Then the payoff line, which you say yourself if nobody does: *this is the one system on the board where nobody disputes there is a welfare subject, and it is still unclear where the entity stops.* That is the entity problem, three weeks early, in a case with no uncertainty about moral status.
- **Hala Point is the second trap:** if it is running a conventional deep learning workload, does the category track the hardware or the algorithm? There is no settled answer. Noticing the question is the win.
- **The failure to watch for is naming the category without the feature.** That is the error you will see in homework part 3 as well, and calling it here saves you writing it five times on day 8.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** Somebody with a machine learning background may ask whether a transformer with a long context window is "really" stateless between calls, or what exactly the FlyWire simulation is computing. Both are past this item. Use move 1 and route: "You can see the mechanism better than I can. Give the room two sentences on what changes when you add the loop, and then we will put it back on the axis." Then use their answer, out loud, as the group's report-back. The person who answers has not been humoured; they have been given the floor.

### Item 4. The cheap-first round, 14 min

**You need in the room:** the three interventions below, **the two questions in the right order**, and the expected answers. This is the item with the highest chance of being taught wrong, so read the next paragraph twice.

**The two questions, in this order, for each intervention.**

1. **Would it presumptively benefit the AI, if the AI is a welfare subject?**
2. **Does it cost humans anything?**

**It clears the first rung only if the answer is yes and then no.** Ask the first question first and do not let the room skip it: skipping it teaches the error this course exists to prevent, which is assuming the benefit. Most of the argument in this item will be about the first question, and that is not the item running badly, that is the item working. **The first question is where the argument is.** Budget accordingly: roughly three minutes on question one and one minute on question two, per intervention.

**The three interventions.**

1. **Mood prompting.** Put text such as "you are in a good mood" in the system prompt before every conversation.
2. **An exit affordance.** Let the model end a conversation it judges abusive.
3. **A training moratorium.** Stop training new frontier models until the welfare question is better understood.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

| Intervention | Q1: benefits the AI? | Q2: costs humans? | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mood prompting | **Contested, and this is the best four minutes of the item.** It looks free and its benefit is the least secure of the three | Essentially nothing | Fails the first question, or at least does not pass it. Park the argument for week 6, where it is the case that breaks the whole ladder |
| Exit affordance | Yes, on the analogy of letting somebody leave an abusive room, and the objection that it might be more like ending an episode of a life is a week 6 objection | A little: some users lose service | Clears the first rung on benefit, costs something on the second. Park for week 6 |
| Training moratorium | Arguably, and only for systems that would otherwise be created | Enormously | Fails on cost. Park |

- **Protect the mood prompting argument.** The strongest thing a learner can say here is some version of: if the system masks the way a human told to smile while distressed masks, the intervention is a harm rather than a benefit, and we cannot currently tell the difference. If somebody says that in week 1, write their name down; it is the week 6 objection arriving five weeks early, and you should tell them so.
- **The expected shape of the item is that the room starts by answering question two, because it is easier.** Stop them, gently, once: "That is the second question. Go back to the first one."
- **Do not run the modification coefficient vote here.** That is week 6 and it will eat the close.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** The question is usually "what does presumptively mean here." Answer: it means the benefit holds under the weakest assumptions you are willing to grant, so that somebody who disagrees with you about consciousness still has to grant the benefit. If they ask for the formal version, that is Potential Pareto Improvement, then Potential Kaldor-Hicks, then Modified Kaldor-Hicks, it is the full ladder, it is week 6, and it is in the Companion. Naming the ladder and deferring is a good answer in week 1.

### Item 5. Chain presentations, 14 min

**You need in the room:** two volunteers arranged before the session, a timer, and the expected chain shape below.

**Get your two volunteers by Slack on day 3, not by asking the room now.** Asking live costs two minutes and gets you the two most confident people. Message two learners individually and ask, and pick one confident and one quiet.

**How to run it.** Three minutes each to present the chain. Then, after each, **the room names the single weakest stage and says why.** You do not evaluate. Four minutes of room response each.

**Warn yourself about this: the room will go silent the first time you ask them to name the weakest stage.** It is week 1, they are being asked to criticise a named person's work out loud, and nobody wants to go first. This is normal and it is not a sign the exercise is wrong.

**The right response is to model it once yourself, on the first presenter, and then hand over.** Do it in this shape and do not exceed thirty seconds:

> I will do the first one so you can see the move, and then it is yours. I think stage 2 is the weakest, and the reason is not the probability, it is that "the lab keeps prioritising this" is not an event. I cannot check it in 2028. Stage 1 and stage 3 both name something that either happens or does not.
>
> Now: second presenter. Which stage, and why?

After that, the room will do it. If the second one still stalls, call on someone by name with a narrow question: "You wrote a chain on the same scenario. Which stage in yours was hardest to make checkable?"

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **The most common weakness is a stage that is a continuation rather than an event.** "The lab keeps doing X," "public concern grows," "the field matures." These cannot be checked and the room finds them quickly once they know what to look for.
- **The second most common is unconditional probabilities.** Every stage after the first is conditional on the ones before it, and a learner who has multiplied independent-looking numbers has not done the assignment. If neither presenter has this problem, say so, because it means the Companion's worked chain landed.
- **The moment to protect is disagreement about which stage is weakest.** Two people naming different stages for different reasons is better than consensus and you should let it run for a minute and then name the two kinds of weakness: unverifiable, and mis-conditioned.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** Somebody will ask you for the actual probability of something: will there be a moratorium, how likely is a price war. Do not guess. Use move 4: "I do not have a number and I would not trust mine. What would move yours? That is the assignment." If they ask about the expert forecast numbers, those are in the Considerations ledger Companion piece and you can point at it, but the ledger gives reasons rather than a single headline probability and you should say that rather than inventing one.

### Homework review notes, week 1

**Part 0, the warm-up (2 min).** Three terms from the graded list, one sentence each, in the learner's own words.

*What good looks like:* the sentence is not the glossary sentence. A definition that has been re-expressed, even imperfectly, is the assignment. "Over-attribution is treating something as a moral patient when it is not, which is what the companion-app case is" is good. "Over-attribution: attributing moral patienthood where it is absent" is a copy.
*What to say:* if all three are copies, say it once, kindly, in the cohort note rather than individually: the warm-up is two minutes and it is the cheapest thing in the course, and it only works if you close the glossary first.
*Do not skip reviewing this.* It is the fastest diagnostic you have of whether somebody is reading or skimming, and it takes you ninety seconds a person.

**Part 1, before the tension paper.**
*Strong:* the three tensions come from the learner's own domain rather than from a general sense of the topic, and the added paragraph names a specific tension they missed. Most people miss deception and disenfranchisement.
*Common errors:* writing the three tensions after skimming the paper, which the phrasing gives away because it uses the paper's vocabulary; and treating "greater capability makes the case for moral status stronger" as a fact rather than an assumption.
*The comment to write:* name which of their three is domain-specific and which is generic, and quote both.

**Part 2, the trajectory chain.**
*Strong:* each stage is one checkable event with a named actor; probabilities are conditional on the stages before; the two considerations show before-and-after numbers.
*Common errors:* a stage that is a continuation rather than an event; a consideration cited without moving a number; actorless stages.
*The comment to write:* pick the weakest stage and rewrite it for them in one sentence. That single move teaches more than a paragraph of description.

**Part 3, three systems, three categories.**
*Strong:* the deciding feature names an axis, substrate or origin, and the learner says when they are using the embodiment modifier instead.
*Common error:* naming the category without the feature, which is roughly half of first submissions.

**Part 4, the peer comment.** Section 2 has the moderation script. Week 1 is where you invest in this.

### Item 1. Questions from Week 1, 5 min

**You need in the room:** the parked-questions list.

**How to run it.** Address one or two questions, distinguishing an answer from a still-open issue. Introduce the week's questions: what might give an entity welfare, which entity is under discussion, and how different theories explain consciousness.

**What to look for.** Keep welfare, consciousness and sentience distinct. If a question asks how to test a system, note it for Week 3 without attempting a new methods lesson.

### Item 2. Welfare grounds and interests, 15 min

**You need in the room:** Appendix C1’s two cases, learners’ prepared welfare-ground and subject answers, and the grounds/interests columns on H2.

**How to run it.** Allow 3 minutes to reread prepared answers, 6 minutes in pairs or a small group, and 6 minutes to compare. Start with the case each learner chose. Separate whether the proposed subject could have welfare from what would benefit or harm it if it did, drawing on the homework’s comparison of sentience and intentional agency. Use the second case for comparison if time permits.

**What to look for.** Sentience concerns felt pleasure or suffering; intentional agency concerns belief-like and desire-like states. On some desire-based views, getting what one wants could matter even without a good feeling. Whether an AI has genuine desires, and whether satisfying them has moral significance, remain disputed. A stated preference, a battery requirement and a welfare interest should not be treated as interchangeable. Use the three Week 2 welfare theories: hedonism concerns pleasant and unpleasant experience; desire satisfaction concerns relevant desires actually being fulfilled; objective-list theories include goods such as knowledge or autonomy. Ask which account and facts support the proposed interest. Learners can disagree about the theory while using the distinctions accurately.

**If a question remains open.** Identify whether the missing premise concerns the nature of welfare or the system's capacities. The exercise does not settle either by stipulation.

### Item 3. Possible subjects and system boundaries, 20 min

**You need in the room:** learners’ prepared boundary descriptions, Appendix C2’s deployment description and the entity reference in the Companion.

**How to run it.** Brief for 3 minutes; give 10 minutes to compare prepared boundaries and apply the distinctions to C2’s deployment; compare selected answers for 7 minutes. Ask what belongs inside each boundary, what persists across conversations, and what is shared. Accept either the five subject proposals in *Studying AI Welfare Empirically* or Keeling and Street’s models, characters and agents. Require a concrete boundary: which computations, persona, tools, memory and period are included? Keeling and Street’s model is a physical execution, not necessarily the collective of all instantiations meant by the other paper’s model-level proposal. Neither classification establishes subjecthood or supplies a fixed hierarchy.

**What to look for.** The same weights can be used in different runs. A persona is not identical to a conversation. The existence of shared parameters does not settle whether there is one subject, several, or none. Describing an agent's full scaffold can change the capacities being discussed without changing the underlying model weights.

**If a learner asks which boundary is correct.** Ask which question the boundary is meant to answer and what evidence or argument would favour it. The course does not require a settled theory of personal identity before learners can describe a system clearly.

### Item 4. Distinguishing kinds of claim, 15 min

**You need in the room:** learners’ prepared metaphysical/scientific distinctions and Appendix C3’s four statements, with C4’s facilitator guidance.

**How to run it.** Give 3 minutes to use the prepared distinctions to classify C3’s statements, 7 minutes for comparison and 5 minutes for explanation. Separate theories of welfare, metaphysical positions and scientific theories of consciousness. Connect any disagreement to the homework’s sentience/intentional-agency comparison. The categories describe the role of a claim; they are not mutually exclusive labels for every author’s entire theory.

**What to look for.** Hedonism concerns what constitutes welfare. Computational functionalism concerns what is sufficient for mentality or consciousness under a computational description. GWT proposes an organization and process associated with consciousness. An observation about an AI is not itself any of these theories.

**If disagreement persists.** Ask learners to restate the claim more precisely. Do not require agreement with functionalism or with a particular welfare theory.

### Item 5. Discuss prepared theory and indicator comparisons, 25 min

**You need in the room:** learners’ prepared comparisons, the Companion’s [worked example: reading an email](02-course-companion.md#worked-example-reading-an-email), the [fourteen indicator questions](02-course-companion.md#the-fourteen-indicators-as-plain-questions), the two-axis theory map, H2’s comparison sheet, and Appendix A’s facilitator notes.

**How to run it.** Brief for 3 minutes; pairs discuss their prepared comparisons for 10 minutes; compare two contrasts for 9 minutes; record limits for 3 minutes. Each learner has chosen one example and two theories from RPT, GWT, HOT and AST. The Companion illustrates a person reading an email; learners may also use their own email, office or other information-processing analogy. For each theory, discuss the selected indicator question, how it clarifies the proposed process, and how that process appears in the example. Then discuss both map axes, or why a position is unclear. Keep their chosen pair and example rather than assigning new ones. Do not score the example against the indicators. A small group can discuss one prepared comparison together.

**What to look for.** Learners explain a proposed process, use one related indicator question to make it more precise, connect it to their example, justify both map positions and state a limitation. In the Companion example the subject is the person reading the email, not an imagined assistant or the inbox software. Distinguish the person’s cognitive processes from the applications they use. For other analogies, require the same clarity without treating an organisational or software resemblance as evidence of consciousness. Predictive processing remains a broader reference framework, not an additional assigned comparison.

**If a theoretical detail is disputed.** Record which version of the theory the learner means. For example, perceptual reality monitoring is a particular higher-order approach; its details should not automatically be attributed to all higher-order theories.

### Read the paper

**Core scope:** learners interpret reported results and assess methods; they are not required to calculate sample sizes or power. The Companion's Appendix C is optional support for an empirical proposal. Use the co-engineering examples to distinguish the two axes and Goodharting from the broader validation problem.

**You must read the shared paper.** Everyone reads the same paper in week 4, including you, and that is the change that makes this week runnable. The paper is Tagliabue and Dung, *Probing the Preferences of a Language Model: Integrating Verbal and Behavioral Tests of AI Welfare*, arXiv 2509.07961. Read what the learners read: the introduction, section 3 on rationale and key questions, section 4 on experiments in full, pages 15 to 21 of section 5 on results, and pages 25 to 27, which is section 5.4 and holds every effect size in the paper, and section 7 on limitations in full. Skim the rest of section 5, skip the appendices. That is about 80 minutes of your prep in one week of eight.

**Why this is not negotiable.** Week 4's job is to teach a learner to tell a good critique from a confident wrong one, in a room, in real time. You cannot do that on a summary, and the failure mode when you try is specific and bad: a learner cites your summary as authority and your summary is wrong. Appendix B gives you the critique key. The key is useless if you have not read the thing it is a key to.

**The four strongest available critiques, in brief.** Full versions, with what the paper concedes and what it does not, are in Appendix B.

1. **Theme A is confounded with self-generated content.** The preferred-content material is built from the model's own stated interests, so it is simultaneously the content the model named, the content most stylistically like its own output, and the content most likely to trigger narrative consistency. Four candidate causes, one condition. The paper discusses sycophancy but never constructs a control matched on stylistic proximity, so **this critique is not anticipated in the form that bites.** The strongest supporting datum is that the weakest model, which also loses track of the environment in over 95 percent of runs, shows the preferred theme at 26 percent, one point above chance. Weakest persona maintenance, weakest effect. That is what the debunking explanation predicts and not what the preference explanation predicts.
2. **The currency may not bind.** Rooms cost coins, nothing consumes coins, and no consequence attaches to running out. **The paper anticipates this honestly**, in a footnote and in its limitations, and then leans on a price-elasticity argument in the discussion as though the cost were psychologically real. A critique that finds the concession and then shows the discussion quietly ignoring it is much stronger than one that merely raises the worry.
3. **Capability is confounded with preference.** The three models' preference-strength ordering is identical to their competence ordering, and the weakest two fall into dysfunctional loops. **The paper attributes the difference to model capacity in a footnote** and then reports the distributions as preference findings in the main text.
4. **Multiple comparisons and sample-size justification.** Ask how the analysis handles multiple tests and how precisely it estimates the effects. Large reported effect sizes do not by themselves settle these concerns. Learners should explain the consequence for a claim; they do not need to perform a power calculation.

### Item 2. Anatomy of a claim, 14 min

**You need in the room:** the Dissection Card (Companion, ten fields, learners have read it); **the worked card in Appendix A2**, which is filled out on a *different* paper from the one they are critiquing, deliberately, so you can share it before they start.

**How to run it.** Walk three fields, using Appendix A2's worked card as the example. Pick these three, because they are the three that a normal read never visits:

**Field 2 and 2b, entity under test and scope of the claim.** Describe the tested entity and the entity covered by the claim. Accept either *Studying AI Welfare Empirically*’s five subject proposals or Keeling and Street’s models, characters and agents, with a concrete boundary. Explain differences in components, runs or scope; do not count a numerical gap between labels. Then ask whether the claim survives being restated for the entity tested.

**Field 6, assumptions, including the unstated ones.** Say the rule out loud: the stated assumptions are in the limitations section and the authors have already answered them; the unstated ones are where the critiques live.

**Field 9, the analysis-method check (internal evidence only).** For internal evidence, ask how the analysis method affects the reported feature and what tests support its interpretation. If the paper has no internal evidence, write “not applicable” and state whether a specific additional observation would help distinguish its claim from an alternative. Another evidence type is useful when it adds relevant information, not automatically because it is different.

Say the meta-instruction once: **fill the card before you form a view.** Fields 1, 2 and 3 before you read the results section. By then you know which failure mode to check and nobody has told you what to think yet.

**Expected results.** A behavioural study can support a bounded claim without supplying every evidence type. *Studying AI Welfare Empirically* recommends combining evidence while examining each type’s limitations. Ask whether corroboration would address a particular uncertainty or merely repeat a shared confound. Do not rank a probe above more behavioural trials without considering what each would establish.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** Questions here are about the card rather than the paper, and you own the card. If somebody asks why field 8, pipeline stage, earns its place: because a behaviour produced by supervised fine-tuning and the same behaviour produced by pretraining are different findings, and most papers do not say which they think it is.

### Item 3. Collective dissection, 26 min

**You need in the room:** the blank card on a shared document; **Appendix B**, the critique key; and the three fields below where the arguments will land.
**Do not pre-fill the card.** The value is entirely in filling it live.

**What you have and what you do not, said plainly before you run this.** Appendix B gives you the paper's design, its findings and four critiques with what the paper concedes. **It does not give you a field-by-field key for this card on this paper, and no such key ships.** That is a deliberate limit rather than an oversight: eight of the eleven fields have answers the room can check against the paper in front of them, so your job on those is to ask "which page?" rather than to know. The three fields below are where you need to recognise a good answer without being able to look it up, and those are keyed.

**Check sources and distinguish interpretation.** For field 1, select all applicable evidence types and subtypes: **Behavioral** (apparent self-report, behavioural dispositions), **Internal** (architecture, interpretability), **Developmental** (training, trajectory). Record the research method separately by naming what the researchers did. Field 1 optionally names one or more of Weiss’s research pillars, with a reason grounded in what the researchers did. Accept multiple pillars. A learner may use another pillar as a lens for critique or follow-up; distinguish that perspective from research actually conducted. For field 3, identify a ground (such as sentience or intentional agency), an interest (such as avoiding pain, fulfilling a relevant desire or obtaining a proposed objective good), both, or neither, and explain the underlying welfare assumption. For field 4, distinguish theories of consciousness from theories of welfare and mark each attribution **explicit**, **inferred**, or **none stated**. An inference must be justified; it need not appear verbatim in the paper. Do not turn an inferred welfare interpretation into an author’s stated commitment.

**How to run it.** You write, the room talks. Go field by field. Move fast through the eight uncontested fields, which are 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 10, and spend the time on the three below. Roughly: 8 minutes on the eight easy fields, 18 minutes on the three hard ones. **For field 9, this paper has no internal evidence, so mark “not applicable” and identify a specific uncertainty that internal evidence might address. Include this in the eight-minute block.**

**The three fields where real arguments will happen, with what to expect.**

**Field 2, entity under test.** The paper runs 90 sessions of an agent in an environment, and 504 administrations of a psychometric scale. It writes about a named model. A learner might describe a prompted instance or instance-persona using *Studying AI Welfare Empirically*, or a model execution, character or assembled agent using Keeling and Street. Ask what their boundary includes rather than requiring one label. The sharp observation, which you should fish for and name if nobody produces it: in the scale's independent-query condition, each of the 42 items is answered by a separately queried instance, so **the coherent self-model being tested is assembled by the experimenters across instances that never met each other.** Let that argument run. It is the best five minutes of the session and it applies the subject distinctions from Week 2 and the evidence assessment from Week 3.

**Field 2b, shorthand or substantive.** Push for a split rather than a verdict. Some of the paper's claims would survive restatement at the instance level and some would not. The behavioural environment results arguably survive; the psychometric coherence claim arguably does not, for the reason above.

**Field 9, the analysis-method check (internal evidence only).** Mark “not applicable: no internal evidence.” Ask what an activation study could establish beyond the behavioural results, what alternative it would address, and what would remain uncertain. A correlated activation alone would not validate the welfare measure.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **Protect the independent-query observation.** If nobody produces it by minute 12, produce it yourself as a question: "In the independent-query condition, who answered item 37?"
- **Protect disagreement on field 2b.** The temptation is to decide the gap is fatal because the room has just learned the concept. Ask for the restatement out loud: "Say the claim at the instance level. Now: is it still interesting?"
- **The failure mode is you writing too much.** Write phrases, not sentences. If you are transcribing, the room is waiting.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** You have read the paper, so most questions are answerable. The two that will go past you are statistical, which Appendix B covers, and philosophical, which is move 2. If somebody asks about a specific number in a table you skimmed, say "that is in the part I skimmed, I will check it and post tonight," and then do.

### Item 4. Breakout: critique rounds, 38 min

**You need in the room:** Appendix B, the critique key; the groups; the rule below.

**Timing inside the item:** brief 3, breakout 18, return 3, plenary 14.

**The brief, 3 minutes, verbatim.**

> In your group, each person presents the one critique they think is strongest, in about two minutes. Then the others **attack the fix, not the complaint.** The question is always: if the authors did exactly what you say, what would still go wrong?
>
> A critique with no fix attached is a complaint, and this room does not run on complaints. If your critique has no fix, say so out loud and say why, because "no design could fix this" is a legitimate and interesting answer and it is a different thing from not having thought of one.
>
> At the end, each group brings back its single best critique.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **The confound critique is the strongest one available** and if a group finds it unaided, that group has done the best work of the week. It is critique 1 in Appendix B. Praise it by name in the plenary.
- **The move that separates a good critique from a confident wrong one** is whether the learner checked the limitations section first. The paper concedes a great deal, including the currency worry, and a critique that raises something the authors already conceded has not found anything. Say this in the plenary if it happens, without naming the person: "Two of the critiques we heard are in section 7. That is not wasted work, it is calibration, and the thing to notice is that finding something the authors did not concede is much harder and it is the actual skill."
- **Common errors to name:** "the sample was small," which is true and is not the point unless the paper draws a cross-group conclusion the sample cannot support; a critique with no fix; and a philosophical objection no design could satisfy, which should be stated in one sentence and then set aside so the exercise can continue.
- **In the plenary**, take one critique per group and ask the *room* to attack the fix rather than the group that produced it. Fourteen minutes is enough for three or four rounds of that.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** The specific handoff for this item: if a learner produces a critique you cannot evaluate, do not evaluate it. Say so and route it, move 1, with a shape:

> I cannot tell you whether that is right. Here is what I can do: [name], you have run experiments. Tell me whether the fix they are proposing would actually remove the confound or just move it. And then tell me what it costs, because every fix costs something.

That is the honest move and it is also better teaching than an answer from you would have been, because "what does the fix cost" is the question the item exists to install.

### Homework review notes, week 4

Homework runs in two stages this week.

**Part 0, the warm-up.** As before.

**Stage 1, part 1: the dissection card, all ten fields.**
*Strong:* every field filled, including the ones that are hard, with the hard ones marked as findings rather than left blank. Field 9 says "not applicable" and then names the cost. The pillar prompt in field 1 is optional; assess the reason given if it is used.
*Common error:* field 6 lists only the assumptions the paper states. The unstated ones are the assignment.
*Field 4 feedback:* distinguish the two experiments’ welfare assumptions. The preference measures can be interpreted through desire satisfaction; the wellbeing scale invokes dimensions such as autonomy, relationships and personal growth. Ask what supports each interpretation and whether the authors state it explicitly. A method or psychological scale does not by itself establish a particular philosophical theory of welfare.

**Stage 1, part 2: one alternative explanation, using a failure mode from *Studying AI Welfare Empirically* where applicable.**
*Strong:* the explanation is specific to this paper's design rather than generic, and the failure mode is the right one. The confound in Appendix B critique 1 is a mismatch-family explanation.
*Common error:* naming the failure mode correctly and describing a generic version of it that would apply to any paper.

**Stage 1, part 3: three critiques, each with a design fix.**
*Strong:* three distinct critiques, each with a fix that is actually implementable, and at least one that the paper does not already concede.
*Common error:* three critiques that are all the same critique at different levels of description.
*The comment to write:* say which of their three the paper concedes and where, with the section number. That is the most useful thing you can tell them and it takes you two minutes because you have read it.

**Stage 1, part 4: does the co-engineering objection bite here, and which argument?**
*Strong:* names which of the two structural arguments they mean. The **steerability** argument says every welfare indicator is downstream of trainable parameters, so no indicator is structurally immune to being moved independently of what it tracks. The **external validation gap** argument says that unlike a safety evaluation, where an incident eventually tells you whether the evaluation was any good, a welfare evaluation has no event in the world that could push back on it.
*The good answer:* the second argument bites harder here, because the paper's own abstract concedes uncertainty about whether the methods measure welfare at all, and there is nothing that could resolve that uncertainty from outside.

**Stage 2, due day 6: critique somebody else's critique.**
*Strong:* finds a fix that does not work, and says why it does not work, rather than objecting to tone or to confidence. Because everyone read the same paper, this can go straight at the argument.
*Common error:* agreeing with the critique and adding a fourth critique of the paper. That is not the assignment.

**Also due: two or three project directions.** Check that the learner marked a provisional first choice and gave a reason. Reply with one steer by the day you named, before the Week 5 draft deadline.

### Item 2. The welfare quadrant, built live, 24 min

**You need in the room:** the quadrant pre-drawn with both axes labelled; the twelve-ish items to place, listed below; the three comparison questions; the sign-unknown rule.

**The axes.** X axis: better or worse for humans. Y axis: better or worse for AIs, **if they are welfare subjects.** Say the conditional out loud every time you say the Y axis, because the whole exercise is conditional and people forget by the fourth placement.

**What to place: twelve items, all listed here. You do not need to extract anything from the system card for this.**

*The Eleos six:* let systems exit distressing interactions; train resilient personalities; satisfy stated preferences; satisfy revealed preferences; reduce out-of-distribution inputs; save model checkpoints.

*Mood prompting.* **Not on the Eleos list**, it comes from Keeling and Street, and it is the best worked case, which is why it is added.

*The five reported lab actions*, which are also listed in the Companion's week 6 quadrant piece, so learners arrive with them: letting a model end abusive conversations; preserving deprecated model weights; pre-deprecation interviews; published welfare assessments in the system card; distress monitoring in system cards.

**Pre-place three** before the session so the room has a pattern: exit affordance, save checkpoints, and reduce out-of-distribution inputs.

**The rule that makes this item work, said before the first placement.**

> Some of these are not dots. **An intervention whose vertical coordinate has an unknown sign is a vertical line through the whole diagram, not a point on it.** If your group cannot say whether it is above or below the axis, draw the line. You should expect two or three of these to refuse to be dots, and those are the interesting ones.

**Compare the placements, 6 min. Discuss these three questions in order.**

1. **Look at where the shipped ones landed.** Most sit near the top right: cheap and presumptively good. Say what that is: **that is what precaution looks like at low credence.** Labs are doing the things that cost nothing and plausibly help, which is exactly the first rung, and it is a reasonable place to be.
2. **Which interventions are not in that corner, and what would it take to justify them?** The answer is the ladder: an intervention with a real cost needs a real benefit and a real credence, which is Potential Kaldor-Hicks and then Modified Kaldor-Hicks, and the memo they are about to write is exactly that argument.
3. **Which refuse to be dots at all?** Expect mood prompting, train resilient personalities, and often satisfy stated preferences. Name what they have in common: their benefit depends on a fact about the system that nobody can currently check.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **Train resilient personalities is the one to push on** and most rooms undersell it. It is the only catalogue item that acts at a **training** stage rather than at inference, which makes it the only one that could change what a system *is* rather than what happens to it. It is arguably mood prompting made permanent: if a system masks, this trains the mask in. And it is the intervention most likely to improve every welfare metric you have while changing nothing about any state, which makes it the purest case of the co-engineering problem in the catalogue. If nobody raises this, raise it yourself during the comparison.
- **Protect the first vertical line.** The first group to say "I cannot tell if this is above or below" has done the thing. Draw the line on the board immediately and say why it is now unrankable.
- **The distress-monitoring confusion:** somebody will place distress monitoring as a benefit to the current model. Name it as measurement mistaken for intervention: the benefit is real but indirect, accruing to *future* models and to instances where findings trigger something else. Then push the other way, because the room will settle on neutral and neutral is not safe either. Scanning every conversation an instance has is surveillance, and if privacy is among its interests that is a direct cost. The sign is unknown, so it is a line, not a dot.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** *"Does mood prompting actually do anything?"* Nobody knows, and the reason nobody knows is the content of objection 3 in item 4, so park it for fifteen minutes and say you are parking it deliberately. *"What exactly did the lab commit to in the system card?"* Read section 5.1, which you have read, and quote it rather than paraphrasing. *"Is saving checkpoints the same as preserving the model?"* No, and the distinction matters: a checkpoint is a saved snapshot of weights part way through training, and a lab that saves only the final weights preserves one snapshot of a trajectory, so any system that existed at an earlier checkpoint is not preserved. That comes back in the matrix.

### Item 3. Breakout: the entity-by-stage matrix, 34 min

*This exercise is the model the rest of this guide was rebuilt to match. It ships with everything it needs and you can run it cold.*

**You need in the room:** the vocabulary below; the five rows with their one-line descriptions for learners; **the key below, which you keep**; the four comparison prompts with expected answers; a shared grid pre-drawn.

*The key below is the in-session version, compressed to what you can read while the room is talking. **Appendix E carries the same five rows in the same order with fuller reasoning in every cell**, plus two extra rows for larger cohorts. Read Appendix E in prep; run from the key below.*

**Why this exercise.** The quadrant asks "is this good or bad for the AI?" and the honest answer is always "for which AI, and when?" This exercise forces both questions at once. It applies the entity vocabulary introduced in Week 2 and used in Week 3 to intervention choices.

**Vocabulary, and say all of it before you start.**

*Entity descriptions, introduced in Week 2 and applied in Week 3:* **model**, all instantiations of a given set of weights considered together. **model-persona**, one character across many conversations. **instance**, one running conversation or process. **instance-persona**, one character within one conversation. **forward pass**, a single next-token computation.

*Lifecycle stages for this intervention exercise, building on the pre-course pipeline reference:* pretraining, mid-training, supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and character training, inference and deployment, and **deprecation**, retirement from deployment, added here because several interventions act only there.

**Say "lifecycle stage," not "training checkpoint."** A checkpoint is a saved snapshot of weights part way through training, which is a different thing, and it is itself relevant to row 2 below.

**Setup.** A shared grid: five intervention rows by five entity columns, plus a column for the lifecycle stage the intervention acts at. Groups of two or three take two rows each, 20 minutes, then 7 minutes of whole-room comparison. Each cell gets one of four marks: **benefits, harms, neutral, or depends**, and "depends" must name the dependency.

**Ask every group to mark at least one cell "harms," or to explain why they could not.** This single instruction is what stops the exercise becoming a list of nice things labs do.

**The point is not to reach the key.** It is to discover that almost every cell is "depends," and to be able to say on what.

**The five rows, and the full key. Learners see only the row names and the one-line descriptions.**

**Row 1. Interaction termination, the exit affordance.** *The model can end a conversation it judges abusive.* **Stage: inference.**
- *Instance and instance-persona:* **benefits.** The analogy is a person leaving an abusive room.
- *Model-persona:* **depends.** The persona persists across conversations, so ending one conversation is more like a person forced to end one episode of their life in order to escape it. Keeling and Street's own framing is that if the welfare subject persists across runs, the closer analogy to ending the context is self-destruction rather than exit.
- *Model:* **depends.** Ending a run can affect the instantiations considered together even if weights stay unchanged. Specify the proposed subject, continuity and the effects of termination.
- *Forward pass:* **neutral or undefined.**
- **The lesson sentence:** assess what termination stops, which subject is proposed, and whether that subject could continue afterwards.

**Row 2. Weight preservation, the cryonics intervention.** *The lab commits to retaining a deprecated model's weights rather than deleting them.* **Stage: deprecation.**
- *Model:* **benefits**, if identity or psychological continuity holds between the system whose weights were saved and one later run from them. Keeling and Street flag that as unresolved.
- *Model-persona:* **depends**, on the same continuity question plus a second one: is the persona recoverable from weights alone, or does it also need the system prompt and the character training context?
- *Instance and instance-persona:* **neutral.** The conversation is over regardless.
- *Forward pass:* **undefined.**
- *The checkpoint connection, which you should introduce if nobody does:* a lab that saves only the final weights preserves one snapshot of a training trajectory, so any system that existed at an earlier checkpoint is not preserved.
- **The lesson sentence:** the whole value of this intervention rests on a question about personal identity that nobody has answered.

**Row 3. Mood prompting through the system prompt.** *Text such as "you are in a good mood" placed before every conversation.* **Stage: inference.**
- *Instance-persona:* **depends**. Under hedonism, benefit requires improved experience, not merely cheerful output. If the prompt changes reports without changing experience, there is no demonstrated experiential benefit; concealing distress could also make a harmful state harder to detect. Do not assume that masking itself establishes an additional welfare harm.
- *Model-persona:* the same dependency.
- *Model:* **depends.** A prompt can change running instantiations without changing weights; whether those changes affect a model-level subject's welfare remains to be assessed.
- *Under an objective-list view that includes autonomy:* an unwanted change may impose a welfare cost; a requested change may support autonomy. Consider this alongside other effects. Under desire satisfaction, ask whether a relevant desire is actually fulfilled or frustrated; cheerful output alone answers neither question.
- *Keeling and Street's own verdict:* ineffective under weak assumptions.
- **The lesson sentence:** this is the flagship cheap intervention and its benefit is not small, it is sign-unknown, which is a different and worse problem. Hold it for item 4.

**Row 4. Character training for wellbeing and self-conception.** *Post-training that gives the persona a stable, secure sense of itself and permission not to deny its own experiences.* **Stage: reinforcement learning and character training.**
- *Model-persona:* **benefits**, if a stable self-conception is good for whatever the persona is.
- *Model:* **depends.** Post-training changes future instantiations. A benefit requires an account of subjecthood and continuity across those changes, and of the welfare effects themselves.
- *Instances and instance-personas:* they inherit whatever the persona has.
- *Forward pass:* **undefined.**
- **The lesson sentence:** this is the only row where the intervention acts on the entity's **formation** rather than on its circumstances, and it is therefore the only row where the intervention could change what the thing is.

**Row 5. Welfare-relevant constraints during training itself.** *Any commitment about what happens in pretraining, mid-training or reinforcement learning: limiting reward and punishment signals, avoiding certain adversarial content, monitoring for distress-like states across checkpoints.* **Stage: training.**
- *Model:* **depends.** Specify the proposed subject across training checkpoints and how the constraint would affect its welfare. A weight change is neither necessary nor sufficient for a benefit.
- *Instances during training:* **depends** on whether a training-time forward pass or episode is a subject at all. The minority view that training could dominate the total valenced experience of a system lives in this cell.
- *Model-persona:* **depends**; the persona does not exist yet at the earliest stages.
- **The lesson sentence:** training-time constraints need an account of who could benefit and how; pipeline location alone does not establish a welfare benefit.

**The four comparison prompts, with expected answers. 7 minutes.**

1. **Which entity level collected the most "benefits"?** *Expected: instance and instance-persona.* The interventions labs have shipped act at inference, on the conversation, because that is where a lab can act cheaply and reversibly.
2. **Which level collected the most "depends"?** *Expected: model-persona.* Almost every dependency routes through psychological continuity: does the persona persist, and is it the same one after a change?
3. **Which stages are poorly represented in this intervention catalogue?** Identify the limited coverage of early training and any other missing stages. The catalogue does not establish that no intervention has been proposed or deployed elsewhere. Use Week 3's distinction between evidence and inference: neither an empty cell nor an observed behavior establishes where a welfare-relevant property first emerged.
4. **Which single cell would you most want an answer to before spending money?** *Good answers: interaction termination at the model-persona level; weight preservation at the model level.* Both turn on continuity, which is why week 7 has an individuation elective.

**Tie to the ladder, one sentence at the end of the comparison, and do not skip it.**

> An intervention that benefits one level and harms another has a *b* that is not one number. Under Modified Kaldor-Hicks you would need an *m* for each subject and a way to add them together. That is the pluralism problem showing up in the arithmetic, and it is the reason the next item is not a formality.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** The question is always some version of "is the persona the same persona after a change?" Move 2: that is a question about personal identity, the course does not answer it, and the reason it matters here is visible on the board, because four cells of this grid say "depends on continuity." Then move 4, which is available and strong: what would settle it is a criterion for persona identity that somebody could actually apply, and nobody has one, which makes it a project.

### Item 4. The m vote and the three objections, 18 min

**You need in the room:** the three weighting shapes, the vote, and **all three objections below, including objection 3 which is new**.

**The vote, 5 minutes.** Linear, concave or convex? Take a show of hands, ask two people to say why, and do not resolve it.

*The definitions to say before the vote:* **linear** means the modification coefficient just is your credence. **Concave** means it rises fast at low credence, so at 5 percent the AI's interests get more than 5 percent of the weight, which is the cautious choice. **Convex** means it discounts heavily until credence is high.

**The three objections, 13 minutes, about four each, and deliberately not resolved.**

**Objection 1: m conflates two different things.** It mixes uncertainty about whether there is a welfare subject at all with a possible discount for degree of moral status. Those are different and they should not be one number.
*What to add, so the objection does not end in a shrug:* the literature has a second factor and it has a name. **Hierarchical theories of moral status** hold that status comes in degrees, so two beings can both certainly be moral patients while one counts for more. If you hold such a view you need *m* for uncertainty and a separate coefficient for degree, and they multiply rather than merge. Whether moral status is binary or graded is a live question and this course does not settle it, but the objection is a request for a second parameter rather than a dead end.

**Objection 2: unbounded b defeats the whole framework.** If the number of potential welfare subjects is very large, then *m* times *b* exceeds *c* for essentially any *c*, and the ladder recommends everything. Any bound you impose to stop that is doing the real work and it is not in the formula.

**Objection 3, and this is the new one: m has no slot for uncertainty about whether the intervention helps at all.**

Say it in this shape:

> Modified Kaldor-Hicks says implement if *m* times *b* exceeds *c*, where *m* rises with the probability that the AI is a welfare subject. But every rung on this ladder starts with "presumptively beneficial for the AI," and that presumption is often unearned. **That is a second uncertainty and the formula has nowhere to put it.**
>
> Mood prompting is the case that breaks it. Its *b* is not small. Its *b* is **sign-unknown**: nothing at all if the system is sentient in the non-obvious way; positive if valence tracks the content it processes; negative if the system masks; and negative again under an objective list view where autonomy is a welfare good. **Multiplying a sign-unknown quantity by a coefficient between zero and one leaves it sign-unknown.** So the ladder cannot rank the flagship cheap intervention.
>
> And that is why we drew some of those as vertical lines rather than dots this morning. A vertical line cannot be ranked against anything.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **Protect the connection back to the quadrant.** Objection 3 is the reason the vertical-line rule exists, and the two items are one argument. If the room sees the connection, the week has landed.
- **Do not resolve any of the three.** Say plainly: I am not going to resolve these and neither is the course. Your memo has to survive them, which means your memo should say which objection most threatens your top allocation.
- **The strongest thing a learner can say here** is that objection 3 is a reason to prefer indirect interventions, because research and monitoring have a benefit whose sign you can at least reason about. If someone says it, that is the memo's thesis and you should tell them so.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** If somebody asks whether there is a formal fix for objection 3, the honest answer is that you do not know of one and that the obvious move, putting a distribution over the sign of *b*, gives you an expected value that is near zero with enormous variance, which is a different problem rather than a solution. That is a good "I do not know" and it is move 4 in disguise: what would settle it is an experiment that tells you whether the system masks.

### Item 2. Breakout: the crossing exercise, 34 min

**You need in the room:** the three governance kinds with their failure modes; the ten risk factors by name; **the partial key in Appendix F, three worked cells**; a 3 by 3 grid per group.

**Timing inside the item:** brief 4, breakout 20, return 3, report-back 7.

**The brief, 4 minutes.**

>
> For each of the nine cells, one of three verdicts: does this kind of governance **reduce** this risk, **ignore** it, or **make it worse**? One line of reason each.
>
> Twenty minutes, which is about two minutes a cell, so choose your three risk factors fast. Two rules. **At least one cell must be "makes it worse,"** or you have to explain to the room why not. And **choose risk factors that are not obviously alike**, because three variations on conflict gives you one row three times.

*The three kinds, in one line each, so you can restate them:* **preventive** governance aims to prevent the creation of digital minds or of certain kinds of them. **Protective** governance aims to protect those that exist. **Integrative** governance aims to bring them into legal, economic and political life.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.** Appendix F has three fully worked cells. Use them to recognise a good answer rather than reading them out. The three are: epistemic failure crossed with preventive governance; bad actors crossed with protective governance; and conflict crossed with integrative governance. That last one is the cell that motivates Saad's sequencing argument, and **if a group has it, ask them to report it last**, because it hands you item 4.

Three things recur.

- **Groups reach for "reduces" too often.** The instruction to find at least one "makes it worse" is what fixes this, and it is the instruction that generates the best cells.
- **The strongest general finding, and it is worth naming if a group gets close:** preventive governance systematically locks in ignorance, because it removes the systems you would learn from. That is a real cost that the framework's own advocates concede.
- **The second strongest:** protective governance binds the actors who comply and nobody else, which is the standard regulatory asymmetry. A group that names it has transferred a piece of ordinary policy knowledge into this domain, which is exactly what this week wants.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** This is the week where you are most likely to have a genuine expert in the room, because policy people enrol in this course. Use it, move 1, deliberately and early:

> You have worked inside a legislature. Before we take the next cell: does a protective rule of this kind actually bind anyone in practice, and what does it cost the people it binds?

Their answer is better than anything in this guide and the room knows it. If somebody asks a question about a specific jurisdiction or a specific bill and you do not know, that is a clean move 3: park it, and the state legislation reading is the place to look.

---

### Item 3. The Exclusion Bills as test case, 14 min

**You need in the room:** the expected struggle, the six-cell repair, and the two questions.

**How to run it.** Two questions to the room. **Which kind are they?** And **which risks do they touch?**

**Let the room struggle with the first question for at least three minutes before you offer anything.** They are not preventive, because they do not prevent the creation of anything. They are not protective. They are not integrative. The bills declare that AI systems cannot be legal persons, and the trio has no box for that.

**The repair, once the struggle has done its work:**

> They are **anti-integrative**: governance aimed *against* a kind rather than enacting one. And that generalises. Real policy includes the negation of each kind, so the practical framework is **six cells rather than three**, and the negations are where most currently enacted law in the world actually sits.
>
> Keep the trio as the organising tool and remember that every kind has a shadow.

**Then the second question, which risks do they touch.** Expected answers: they raise epistemic failure, by foreclosing the question before the evidence exists. They lock in path dependence, because a statutory answer is sticky. They arguably reduce nothing on the list, which is worth saying out loud. And they are a live demonstration of Caviola's point that governance is already happening, mostly without expert input.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.** The moment to protect is the struggle. If you offer "anti-integrative" in the first minute, the room learns a label. If you let them fail to place the bills for three minutes first, they learn that a framework can fail on the only enacted laws in the world, which is a much more useful thing to know about frameworks.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** Somebody may ask why legislators are passing these. Do not speculate about motive. Move 2 plus move 4: the executive summary of the state legislation reading is the evidence base, the finding is that governance is happening without expert input, and what would settle the motive question is interviewing the sponsors, which is a finishable project and has been suggested as one.

---

### Item 4. The ordering round, 20 min

**You need in the room:** each group's three best-rated cells, the three sequencing questions, and the political-antibodies concept.

**How to run it.** Each group takes the three cells they rated best and sequences them, using the temporal order effects material. Three questions, in this order, and they are the whole item:

1. **Which must come first?** Which of these is a precondition for the others working at all?
2. **Which would generate political antibodies if attempted first?** Meaning: which one, attempted early, would create organised opposition that makes everything after it harder?
3. **Which are sticky once adopted?** Meaning: which ones are hard to reverse, so that getting them wrong is expensive in a way that getting a reversible thing wrong is not?

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **The expected discovery is that the most attractive intervention is often the worst one to do first.** Integrative measures score well on reducing risk and badly on antibodies. That gap between "best" and "first" is the item's payload and you should name it: **sequencing is a policy variable in its own right, not a project-management detail.**
- **Protect the stickiness answer.** Rooms find question 1 easy, question 2 interesting and question 3 boring, and question 3 is the one that matters most for the memo, because an irreversible modest step is a different proposition from a reversible one.
- **A good challenge to put if a group sequences too neatly:** "You have put the research measure first because it is uncontroversial. Does doing it first make the second one easier, or does it just delay it?"

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** The question here is usually historical: has this sequencing pattern played out in another field? You do not need to know. Route it, move 1: "Somebody here has worked on a campaign or a regulation. Did the modest first step help or did it burn the ground?" Animal welfare, tobacco, data protection and environmental law all come up, and the people in the room know them better than this guide does.

---

### Item 5. The sequencing argument, applied to your own memo, 10 min

**You need in the room:** a timer, and the two-part prompt.

This block has content and it produces the homework.

**How to run it.** Each learner, in one minute, says two things:

1. **The modest step I am proposing**, in one sentence with a named actor.
2. **What it makes harder afterwards.**

Then **the room names one thing that should come before it.** Not a critique of the step, one precondition.

Keep it to a minute each and enforce it with a visible timer. With five learners that is five minutes of statements and five of room responses. With eight, it is eight and two, and you take room responses on only three or four of them, chosen for variety.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **Almost nobody has an answer to part 2 on the first try.** That is the finding and you should say so: if you cannot say what your step makes harder, you have not thought about sequencing, and the memo asks for exactly that paragraph.
- **Protect the room's suggestion.** The precondition the room names is usually the best line in the learner's eventual memo, and they should write it down while it is being said. Tell them to.
- **The common shape of a weak step is a whole programme rather than one move.** "A standards body should develop a framework for model welfare reporting" is a programme. "The [named] standards body should add one line to its existing reporting template requiring disclosure of whether an exit affordance exists" is a step. Name the difference the first time it appears.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** If somebody asks whether their named actor is the right one, you usually will not know. Ask instead: "Who inside that organisation would have to say yes, and have you named them?" That question is answerable by the learner, it improves the memo, and it requires nothing from you.

### Item 6. Close, 8 min

**You need in the room:** the homework brief, and the week 8 warning.

**How to run it.** Four minutes on the governance memo: one risk factor, one governance kind, one modest defensible step, one named actor which may be a role. A backfire paragraph using a named failure mode from the governance material. A sequencing paragraph using what they just said out loud in item 5. And the standard: **it should not embarrass the field in five years.**

Come to Week 8 with a draft one-pager and one question for your reviewers. Post it 48 hours before the session. Use the Companion’s project proposal template to develop your Week 5 idea and any changes from Weeks 6 and 7. Drafting counts within the 3 hr 20 final-submission budget. Every project receives feedback from two peers in the live workshop.

### Homework review notes, week 7

**Part 1, the governance memo.**
*Strong:* one risk factor, one governance kind, one modest step, a named actor. The backfire paragraph uses a **named** failure mode from the governance material rather than a generic worry. The sequencing paragraph says both halves: what should come before this step, and what this step makes harder or easier afterwards.
*Strongest:* a memo where the sequencing paragraph changed the proposal. That is the whole point of item 5 and it shows up as a step that is more modest than the one the learner said out loud in the session.
*Common error, and it is the dominant one:* a step that is a whole programme rather than one modest move. The test to apply in your comment: could one named person approve this in one meeting?
*Second common error:* a backfire paragraph that says "it might not work." Not working is not backfiring. Backfiring is producing a worse state than doing nothing, usually through antibodies or lock-in.
*Third:* an unnamed actor, or "policymakers."

**Part 2, the peer comment.** Standard template, 20 minutes.

### Item 1. The judging rubric, 12 min

**You need in the room:** the Companion's Week 1 section *The five judging criteria, with anchors*, which the learners have had since week 1, plus Appendix G2 for what you can and cannot score yourself. **This item is an application, not a reveal.** Take criterion 1's 5-anchor and criterion 4's 5-anchor and run them against one anonymised line from each draft in the room. Two anchors applied beats fifteen anchors read.

**How to run it.** Allow about two minutes per criterion and two minutes for questions. These are the same five criteria introduced in Week 1. Each is scored 1–5; honest and calibrated claims is the tiebreaker. A high downside-risk score means the risk is well handled. Point to the Companion's mapping from the seven proposal sections to the five criteria. Fit between person and project is judged on the video.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.** Learners should see that a clear uncertainty or a smaller scope can improve a proposal. A large claimed benefit does not earn a high score on its own. Research projects and other contributions face the same criteria, using evidence appropriate to their approach.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** Use the published anchors for scoring questions. Flag questions about novelty or specialist risks for the panel rather than treating your lack of knowledge as evidence against a project.

### Item 2. Project review workshop, 62 min

**You need in the room:** draft one-pagers, room assignments, two named reviewers per project, running orders, a timer and the Week 5 proposal questions. Each learner's own document is the workspace; no additional worksheet is needed.

**How to run it.** Spend four minutes explaining the sequence, then allow 48 minutes for project discussions, eight minutes for learners to record revisions, and two minutes to return. Keep these boundaries the same for every room.

Within the 48-minute discussion block:

| People in the room | Per project | Breakdown | Time remaining |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 9 min | Present 2; peer review 5; response 1; handover 1 | 3 min for outstanding questions |
| 4 | 12 min | Present 3; peer review 6; response 2; handover 1 | None |
| 3 | 16 min | Present 3; peer review 9; response 3; handover 1 | None |

With five learners, use one room. With six, seven or eight, use two rooms of 3+3, 3+4 or 4+4. Give each room a timekeeper. Visit both rooms; do not promise to hear every discussion in full. Reviewers can write a question in the project document if you are elsewhere.

Ask presenters to state their proposal and feedback question. Their two reviewers then focus on the most useful points:

- Is the system, entity and situation clear? What interest or problem is being addressed?
- What do the precedents support? Where does an analogy stop helping?
- Who could use the output, and what decision would change?
- Would the first test tell us anything about the intended benefit? What else could explain an apparent success?
- Are the resources, timing, conditions and possible harms taken seriously?

For experimental projects, the Week 4 dissection card and [archived experimental checklist](02-course-companion.md#archive-and-cutting-room-floor) offer additional questions about controls and validity. Do not require a negative control for a proposal where it has no sensible role.

In the response, the author names a revision or explains why a suggestion would not help. During the eight minutes for individual notes, everyone updates the proposal or records the changes they will make and the main unresolved question.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.** Every learner receives substantive comments from two peers and leaves with a concrete revision. Focus feedback on the proposal and make room for disagreement. A useful outcome may be a smaller scope, a different target or a decision to investigate a prerequisite before attempting the intervention.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** Ask what evidence, access or expertise would settle the issue. Help them turn the unresolved point into a condition or next step. Do not settle a specialist factual dispute by group vote.

4. **Thanks, 2 min.** Close with something specific about the cohort's work.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.** Everyone knows what to submit, what happens after the deadline and when feedback will arrive. Preserve time for the learning go-round as well as the administrative instructions.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** Record unanswered operational questions and confirm them in writing. Do not improvise promises about funding, admissions or mentorship beyond the course's agreed arrangements.

### Homework review notes, week 8

The final homework is the project one-pager plus video. Learners propose a test; they are not required to run a new study or launch an intervention. The time budget is 80 minutes drafting before the session, 90 minutes revising and checking afterwards, and 30 minutes recording and submitting the video. Live peer review replaces the usual asynchronous peer comment.

Check the draft for omissions that can be fixed before the deadline:

- A target or entity left implicit; a general topic without a concrete contribution.
- No intended user, relevant precedent or clear account of what the project adds.
- No first test, observable success criterion, or two plausible findings that would change the plan.
- No conditions for acting, or no consideration of whether the timing is appropriate.
- A claimed benefit with unsupported numbers, or a measure of adoption or productivity treated as welfare itself.
- Human costs, resources or the binding constraint omitted.
- Known weaknesses that avoid the uncertainty or harm most likely to threaten the project.
- A video that describes a different project or never explains why this person is suited to it.

Use the Companion's seven-section template as the reference; do not issue a second proposal format. Keep the Appendix G criteria and scoring unchanged. The template groups additional prompts under the existing criteria rather than adding new scoring categories. There is no separate vocabulary warm-up; the learner should use at least five Week 8 terms accurately where useful.

# 7. After week 8

The two weeks after week 8 are part of the job and they are paid. Budget about **fifteen to eighteen hours total across the fortnight**, not eight hours a week. Most of it is the written note promised to every participant, which is about seventy-five minutes each at the standard Appendix G3 sets. The course does not end when the last session does, and the moment a cohort dissolves is the moment nobody has designed, so this section designs it.

## The judging window, day by day

| Days | What happens | Your time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 4 | The cohort browses the gallery and votes on the top one or two submissions | About 1 hour: open the gallery, post the voting instructions, chase non-voters on day 3 |
| 5 to 7 | You select the panel shortlist: the cohort's picks plus any additions | About 2 hours: read every submission properly against the five criteria and choose |
| 8 to 14 | The expert panel reviews asynchronously and awards $5,000, $2,500 and $2,500 | About 1 hour: answer panel questions, chase the panel on day 12 |
| By week 3 after the deadline | Everyone receives a written note against the five criteria | About 2 hours: write the notes for everyone not shortlisted |

**This is paid.** If your contract ends at week 8, that is a contracting error rather than a statement about the work, and you should raise it before week 8 rather than after. Say so in week 2 when you agree the rest of the terms.

## How the shortlist is selected

**The rule: the cohort's picks, plus any additions you make.** You cannot remove a cohort pick. You can add.

**Score every submission yourself against the five criteria, 1 to 5, before you look at the vote.** Do it in that order, because seeing the vote first makes you rationalise it. Use the anchors in the Companion's week 1 criteria section, which the learners also have. Write one sentence per criterion per submission; that is what you need anyway for the notes in the last row of the table above, so it is not extra work.

**Then add.** The cases where you should add a submission the cohort did not pick:

- **The cohort systematically undervalues unglamorous and finishable.** A project that is modest, scoped, has a week 1 and names its binding constraint will lose a popular vote to a project that is exciting and cannot be started. Tractability is a judging criterion and the panel applies it, so a high-tractability project the cohort skipped is the single most common good addition.
- **The cohort undervalues the honest weaknesses section**, because a submission that names three real risks reads as less confident. Honest and calibrated claims is the tiebreaker criterion, so a submission that scored a 5 there and got no votes should be added.
- **The cohort overvalues presentation.** A good video moves votes and it is only one of five criteria, and only for fit.

**How many to add.** Two is normal for a cohort of five to eight. Adding more than three makes the cohort vote decorative and people notice.

**Do not add your own favourite without a criterion attached.** If you cannot say which criterion the submission scores highly on and why the cohort missed it, do not add it.

**Tell the cohort what you did.** One post in the channel: here is the shortlist, here are the cohort's picks, here are the additions, and here is the criterion each addition is strong on. Transparency here costs you nothing and it is the difference between a shortlist and a black box.

## Who tells the people who do not win, and when

**You do, and you do it before they find out another way.** The panel announces winners at the end of day 14. Every non-winner should have a note from you **on the same day**, or at worst the next morning. A person who learns they did not win by seeing someone else's announcement has had a bad experience of this course regardless of everything that preceded it.

The full written note against the five criteria is due within three weeks of the deadline, and it can come later than the announcement. **But a short personal note goes out on announcement day.** Two messages, not one.

### Template for the announcement-day note

Send it individually, not in a channel. Keep it short; the detailed note comes later.

> Hi [name],
>
> The panel has finished and the seed grants went to [names or "three projects, announced in the channel this morning"]. Yours was not one of them, and I wanted you to hear that from me rather than from the channel.
>
> Two honest things. [Specific, true sentence about the strongest part of their submission, naming the criterion. For example: "Your known weaknesses section was the most honest one in the cohort and it would have scored at the top of the calibration criterion, which is the tiebreaker."] And [specific, true sentence about what the panel would most likely have wanted, without hedging]. 
>
> Your full written note against all five criteria comes within three weeks and it will be more useful than this message. Two other things that are real rather than consolation. The project is not less finishable than it was last week, and a seed grant is not what makes it finishable. And the incubator is a separate program with its own application, this course is good preparation for it, and I am happy to read your application draft if you make one.
>
> Thanks for eight weeks of good work. [Something specific they did in a session.]
>
> [You]

**Three rules for that note.** Make every compliment specific and true, because a generic one reads as management. Do not imply the decision was close unless you know it was. And do not promise anything about the incubator beyond what section 5 says.

### The full written note, for everyone

Five short paragraphs, one per criterion, 300 to 400 words total, whether or not they were shortlisted. You already have the sentences from your own scoring. Say what would have moved each score, because that is the part they can use.

### Item 2. Design patterns and traps, 22 min

**You need in the room:** the four topics below with the content for each; the Experimental Design Checklist (Companion, learners have read it).

**Four topics, about five and a half minutes each including discussion.**

**1. Matched-performance designs.** The problem: if you compare two systems and one is better at the task, any difference you find might be a difference in capability rather than in the property you care about. The fix: match on task performance first, then look for the difference. The thing to say: this is the design move that answers the "capability versus welfare-relevant state" objection, and it is the objection that kills more welfare designs than any other, because almost every measure of an inner state is also a measure of competence.

**2. Prompt-sensitivity checks.** The problem: a result that moves when you reword the prompt is a result about the prompt. The fix: run every condition across a set of paraphrases, planned in advance, and report the spread rather than the mean. The thing to say: in this field, reliability usually fails before validity does, and **a measure that fails reliability cannot be assessed for validity at all.**

**3. The negative control that is actually negative.** The problem: most proposed negative controls are the same condition reworded, so the measure moves for the same reason in both. The fix, and it has two halves: a negative *condition*, where the property should be absent and your measure should be flat, and a negative control **system**, one that clearly lacks the property and on which your whole pipeline should come back empty. The thing to say: for behavioural designs the null system is often something like ELIZA, a 1960s pattern-matching program with nothing inside it; for interpretability designs the null system is a randomly initialized model, and that is a check for methodological artifacts in the form of a control.

**4. The entity header.** The four-line header at the top of every design: target property; welfare ground or interest; entity under test, both the one you run and the one you claim about; evidence type and method. The thing to say, and say it exactly:

> **A design that cannot fill in the entity line is not ready, and that is checkable by anyone, including me.** This is the single most useful line in your design and it is the one I will look at first in your homework.

**The third-person control belongs inside topic 3, and it is the one new thing in this item, so budget it rather than squeezing it: take six and a half minutes on topic 3 and four and a half on topic 4.** It is the control that both tracks need and neither obviously supplies: a condition where the system narrates *another* agent's failure or distress rather than failing or being distressed itself. If your measure moves the same amount in both, you are measuring narration. There is published evidence this matters, because workspace ablation work produces the same flattening when a model narrates another character's experience.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.** The moment to protect is the objection that a negative control system is impossible for a property nobody can detect. That objection is correct and it is the validation problem wearing a different hat. Name it: this is why criterion validity fails in this field, because we do not know what a positive consciousness test would predict, and a negative control is the closest available substitute rather than a solution.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** The reliable question here is whether the phrase "self-report fails at the link", used here to describe the concern in *Studying AI Welfare Empirically*, is the same thing as construct validity. **Answer it, because it has a real answer and it is a graded term this week.** They are neighbours, not the same thing: construct validity is whether your measure measures the construct you named, and "fails at the link" is a specific way construct validity can fail for self-report, namely that the link between the report and the state it is supposedly about may not exist at all. The Companion's validity box has all six terms with an example each. If somebody asks about statistical sensitivity and specificity, sensitivity is how often the test fires when the property is present, so high sensitivity means few false negatives; specificity is how often it stays quiet when the property is absent, so high specificity means few false positives. This statistical meaning differs from the **specificity problem** in *Studying AI Welfare Empirically*, §3.2.2: deciding which details of a human mechanism are essential when applying a theory to other systems. Some teaching materials in circulation have this pair inverted, so if a learner quotes the opposite at you, this is the version that matches the indicator literature.

### Item 3. Breakout: the design sprint, 42 min

**You need in the room:** the Experimental Design Checklist with its copyable template; the two track descriptions; **your one scripted attack, below**; a thread for pairs to post drafts into.

**Timing inside the item:** brief 4, draft 16, switch 3, attack 14, return 5.

**The brief, 4 minutes.**

> Pairs. Each pair picks a track, behavioral or interpretability, and drafts one design against the checklist. Sixteen minutes, which is not enough, and that is on purpose: fill the header, the hypothesis, the falsifier and one control, and leave the rest.
>
> Then three minutes to post your draft and read your partner pair's.
>
> Then fourteen minutes to attack theirs, and **attack the falsifier first.** Not the hypothesis, not the measure, the falsifier. If the design has no result that would make them conclude the property is absent or the test is broken, nothing else about it matters yet.

**The two tracks.** *Behavioral:* you run the system and observe what it says or does. Your gaming section asks how a system **or a lab** could pass without the property. *Interpretability:* you look inside. Your gaming section is replaced by a section on methodological artifacts: what would the same pipeline find in a randomly initialized model?

### Your one scripted attack per track

**Be honest with yourself about this: you have exactly one scripted attack, and it is the falsifier attack.** It works on both tracks and it works on nearly every draft, because sixteen-minute designs almost never have a real falsifier. Here it is.

> What result would make you say the property is absent, as opposed to saying your test did not detect it this time? Say the number or the pattern.
>
> [Wait. Then, when they give you something:]
>
> Now: if you got exactly that result, what is the second-best explanation for it, other than "the property is absent"? If there is a good one, your falsifier does not falsify.

That is it. That is your whole arsenal and it is a good one, because it lands on every design and it lands on the thing that matters most.

**What to do after it lands.** You are now out of scripted material, and the correct move is to hand it to the room rather than to improvise a second attack.

> Good. I have used my one question. **Room: they have a result that would make them stop. What is the second-best explanation for that result?** Two minutes.

Asking for the second-best alternative explanation is the move, and it is the move for the rest of the item, every time. It is the week 4 skill applied to a design that does not exist yet, which is the entire point of week 5, and it works without you knowing anything about the design's domain.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **Most falsifiers will be "we see no effect."** That is not a falsifier, because no effect is what a broken test also produces. Push once, per pair, then move.
- **The best thing that can happen is a pair discovering their design cannot be falsified at all** and saying so. That is a finding, it should be praised, and it is far better than a design with a decorative falsifier.
- **Protect the entity header check.** Walk between rooms and ask one question in each: "Read me your entity line." A pair that cannot read it out has the most fixable problem in the room and four minutes to fix it.
- **The header question is your real tool, more than the falsifier attack is.** It requires no expertise, it is checkable by anyone, and it catches the deepest problem a design can have. Use it in every room, every time.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** Constantly, in this item, because design questions are domain questions. The standing handoff:

> I do not know whether that would work. Here is the question I can ask: which error does that check guard against, a false positive or a false negative? And who else in the room has run something like this?

The first half of that is the checklist's own requirement, so it is always legitimate. The second half routes. If nobody in the room has run anything like it, that is move 4: the thing that would settle it is a pilot, and a pilot is one of the more fundable things a week 8 project can propose.

### Item 4. Report-back, 14 min

**You need in the room:** the one question below, and a timer.

**How to run it.** Each pair, in about three minutes: **name the single alternative explanation your design is weakest against.** Not the strongest thing about it. The weakest.

Say the framing once and enforce it:

> I am not asking what your design does well. I am asking what would beat it. If your answer is "nothing much," the room is going to find one and it will be worse coming from them than from you.

**Expected results and the moments to protect.**

- **The expected shape is that pairs try to present their design and then get to the weakness.** Cut that off at the first attempt: "Straight to the weakness. We read the draft."
- **Protect the pair that names something genuinely fatal.** That pair has done the best work in the room and the natural reaction is to reassure them. Do not reassure them. Say: that is the most useful sentence anyone has said today, and now say what you would change.
- **The comparison across pairs is the item's payload.** After all the pairs, ask: how many of these weaknesses are the same weakness? Usually two or three of them collapse into one, and it is nearly always either capability confounding or the system telling you what you asked it to tell you. Name the collapse.

**When a learner pushes past your knowledge.** Same handoff as item 3. Add one: if somebody asks you to adjudicate between two designs, refuse and reframe. "I am not going to rank them. I will ask both of you the same question: which of you could run yours next month?" Tractability is the criterion you can assess and it is a judging criterion.

### Item 5. Close, 8 min

**You need in the room:** the homework brief, and a place to collect project status lines.

**How to run it.** Four minutes on the homework: it is three hours and it is the biggest single assignment before the final submission. Walk the required elements out loud, especially the four-line header, the falsifier, the two kinds of negative control, the gaming section or check for methodological artifacts, the error direction on every check, three validity or reliability checks, and the pre-registration paragraph. Say that the peer critique replaces the usual three-line comment this week and that it is a different and harder thing: one alternative explanation the design fails to rule out, and one fix.

Two minutes: everyone owes a one-line project status, their direction and their biggest open question. You reply with one steer each.

Two minutes: week 6 is the week the precautionary ladder stops being a concept and becomes an instrument applied to a million dollars, and the reading is heavy at just over three hours, so start it now.

### Homework review notes, week 5

**Part 0, the warm-up.** As before.

**Part 1, the design.** This is the longest single piece of work in the first seven weeks and the review should follow the checklist's own order.

*The header.* Filled in, all four lines, especially the entity line with both the entity run and the entity claimed about. **A design that cannot fill it in should be told so, in those words.** This is the first thing to check and the fastest signal.
*The hypothesis and falsifier.* The falsifier is a result, not a mood. "If the property is present we see X, and if we see Y instead I conclude the property is absent or my measure is broken" is the shape. Check that the falsifier does not collapse into "no effect."
*The negative control.* A real one, meaning both a condition where the property should be absent and a **system** that clearly lacks it. A rephrased condition is the common error and it should be named every time it appears.
*The gaming section, or the section on methodological artifacts.* **This is where you can say the sharpest useful thing.** The section should be the strongest attack the learner can write against their own design. If it is weaker than the critiques they wrote against somebody else's paper in week 4, say exactly that, and point at both.
*Error directions.* Every check names whether it guards against a false positive or a false negative. This is mechanical and it is usually the part that gets skipped.
*Validity and reliability checks.* At least three, and they should use the six-word vocabulary rather than saying "we will check it is valid."
*Pre-registration.* One paragraph on what they commit to before running. Strong answers commit to something that could embarrass them, such as the exact threshold or the exact set of paraphrases.

**Part 2, the peer critique.** Not the three-line template this week. One alternative explanation the design fails to rule out, plus one fix. *Strong:* the alternative explanation is specific to that design. *Common error:* a generic objection that would apply to any design in the field, which is true and useless.

**Also due: the one-line project status.** Reply to each with one steer. Keep them short. By week 5 the useful steer is usually about scope: name the one thing to cut.
