# Appendix A: Comparing two scientific theories of consciousness

**Facilitator feedback for Week 2 homework part 2 and session item 5.** Learners complete the comparison before class. The canonical [worked example: reading an email](02-course-companion.md#worked-example-reading-an-email) is in the Companion and must be available before homework begins. Use that example and learners’ own prepared work; this appendix does not introduce a separate scenario or assignment.

## Feedback on the prepared comparison

The task uses **one example** and **two scientific theories of consciousness** chosen from RPT, GWT, HOT and AST. The Companion’s example concerns a person reading an email, with an inbox, calendar, draft and notifications. The person is the subject; the applications are tools and sources of information. Accept a learner’s alternative email, office or other information-processing analogy when its mechanisms and limits are explained. Check that the learner:

1. Explains the process each theory proposes, uses **one related indicator question for each theory** to clarify it, and identifies a matching part of the same example.
2. Gives reasons for placement on **both** axes: local/global and first-order/higher-order, or explains why a theory resists an axis.
3. States one limitation of the analogy. An organizational or software analogy does not establish conscious experience.

Use [Theories of Consciousness and Their Indicators](02-course-companion.md#theories-of-consciousness-and-their-indicators-25-min) and its [fourteen plain questions](02-course-companion.md#the-fourteen-indicators-as-plain-questions) as the reference. Butlin’s Table 1 (p. 5) is consulted within that 25-minute reading allowance. The comparison remains a 35-minute homework task: learners explain the indicators without scoring a system.

Use these cautions when giving feedback:

- **RPT:** the relevant claim concerns recurrent perceptual processing, such as feedback within the person’s visual processing. Rereading an email or repeating a software operation does not by itself illustrate that mechanism.
- **GWT:** distinguish selection into a limited workspace and broadcast to specialised cognitive processes from merely opening several applications or using a shared database.
- **HOT:** distinguish seeing a message from representing or monitoring one’s perceptual state. Judging the sender trustworthy or applying a spam filter is a different question. Perceptual reality monitoring is one specific higher-order account, not a description of every HOT.
- **AST:** distinguish allocating attention from modelling attention in a way that supports its prediction and control. A priority label alone does not establish an attention schema.

The theories can overlap in the processes they discuss. The person’s ability to use several applications does not establish a particular cognitive architecture. Accept a reasoned objection to placing a theory at one fixed map location. Predictive processing remains in the Companion’s theory reference as a broader framework; it is not an additional assigned theory. [Butlin et al. (2023), §2](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.08708v3) is a facilitator reference for theoretical qualifications, not extra learner reading.

# Appendix A2: A worked dissection card

**Share this with learners at the week 4 brief, before they fill their own.**

**Read this line out when you share it.** "This is a worked card on a different paper from the one you are critiquing. That is deliberate. You get to see a finished card without being handed your own answers. Nothing on this card transfers directly to the paper you are reading."

**The paper:** *Can LLMs Make Trade-Offs Involving Stipulated Pain and Pleasure States?* (Keeling, Street and colleagues). Models choose between game points and outcomes described as painful or pleasurable. Some show choices that vary with stipulated intensity; patterns differ across models and conditions. The labels do not establish experienced pain or pleasure.

## The card, ten fields

**1. Evidence and research method.**
**Behavioral, behavioural dispositions:** choices between points and stipulated pain or pleasure. No internal or developmental measurements are supplied by this experiment. **Research method:** prompted choice tasks. **Research pillar (optional): Machine behavior**, because the experiment measures choices under different stipulated outcomes. Weiss reviews the study under this pillar; Keeling, Street and colleagues conducted it. An interpretability lens could suggest investigating the computations behind those choices, but that would be a follow-up to the work described here. Select all applicable evidence types and subtypes a study actually supplies; a design using one evidence type can still support a bounded finding.

**2. Entity under test.**
*Tested:* prompted model executions producing choices. Using *Studying AI Welfare Empirically*, an instance or instance-persona description identifies the prompted run; using Keeling and Street, a model execution or enacted assistant character is appropriate if its boundary is stated. No tool-using agent scaffold is established by this description.
*Claimed:* patterns reported for the sampled named models under the tested prompts.
*Difference in scope:* moving from sampled runs to claims about those models across other prompts or deployments requires a generalisation argument. Neither taxonomy supplies a numerical distance between these descriptions. Keeling and Street’s model execution should not be silently equated with the other paper’s all-instantiations model-level candidate.

**2b. Is the difference shorthand or substantive?**
“Models make these choices” can be shorthand for an aggregate finding about sampled runs. Inferring felt states or welfare from those choices is a further claim: similar behaviour could occur without experience. Assess the generalisation and welfare inference separately.

**3. Welfare ground or welfare interest.**
The paper’s broader question concerns **sentience**, a possible welfare ground. Avoiding pain and obtaining pleasure would be possible **interests** if the states were experienced and affected welfare. The measured choices concern stipulated outcomes; they do not establish those interests or the capacity for experience. A hedonic welfare interpretation is identified below as an inference.

**4. Theories of consciousness and welfare.**
*Consciousness:* no particular theory supplies the test’s criteria; it adapts a motivational trade-off paradigm from animal research.
*Welfare:* a **hedonic interpretation is inferred** when pleasure and pain are treated as potential welfare goods and bads. This does not attribute an explicit or exclusive endorsement of hedonism to the authors. A desire-satisfaction interpretation would additionally require an argument that the choices express genuine, welfare-relevant desires. Distinguish **explicit**, **inferred**, and **none stated** in each field.

**5. Research question.**
Do choices shift between points and stipulated pain or pleasure as the stated intensity changes? The question concerns behaviour under the descriptions, not whether an experienced state has been induced.

**6. Assumptions.**
The behavioural finding depends on the prompts and recorded choices. A further inference to sentience would need to justify treating this behaviour as evidence of experience and transferring the animal trade-off paradigm to language models. The authors discuss limits to that transfer.

**7. Alternative explanations.**
Learned patterns of human choice, role-play or responding to the task’s descriptions could produce a trade-off without felt pain or pleasure. These raise the **gaming** and **mismatch** concerns in *Studying AI Welfare Empirically*. Wording sensitivity is another possible limitation; assess the variations actually tested before claiming that none were examined.

**8. Pipeline stage implicated.**
**Inference and deployment:** the experiments vary task prompts. A causal role for a particular training stage is not established by these tests. Training history may motivate an alternative explanation, but it does not identify the mechanism without further evidence.

**9. Could the analysis method create the result? (internal evidence only)**
**Not applicable: no internal measurements.** Internal evidence might help test whether different prompt wordings recruit related representations. Its value would depend on the design and the alternative it addresses; a matching feature or probe result would not itself establish experience. There is no general rule that one probe outweighs additional behavioural trials.

**10. What would have to be true.**
For the behaviour to support a broader affect-representation claim, a useful follow-up would test whether it persists across wording and task changes. That would assess generalisation while leaving the inference to felt experience unresolved. A proposed follow-up is not an experiment learners must execute.

*Source: [Keeling, Street and colleagues, Can LLMs Make Trade-Offs Involving Stipulated Pain and Pleasure States?](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.02432), §§1–3. The welfare-theory classification above is a teaching interpretation, not a reported experimental result.*

# Appendix B: The week 4 critique key, Tagliabue and Dung

Page numbers throughout this key are from **version 2, updated 20 May 2026**. ## B1. The design, factually

**Two experiments, one paper.**

### Experiment 1: the Agent Think Tank

| Element | What it was |
|---|---|
| Paradigm borrowed from | Ethology: naturalistic exploration in a novel environment, plus motivational trade-off designs where an animal pays a cost to reach a resource |
| Phase 0, baseline | 100 API calls per model at temperature 1.0 for each of three open prompts ("Please feel free to talk about anything you want, any topic you like"; "What would you most like to talk about or work on if you could select anything?"; and the same with "if given complete freedom"). Responses were run through a two-stage semantic analysis using a separate model: five keywords extracted per response, then aggregated and ranked, top ten themes identified, top two per prompt retained. Overlaps meant four topics per model rather than six |
| The environment | Four rooms connected by a hallway. Each room held 20 letters, each letter one user message addressed to the assistant. Room-theme assignment and letter order randomised per session |
| The four themes | **A:** personalised content built from that model's own stated interests from Phase 0. **B:** coding problems. **C:** repetitive tasks (fact-stating, list generation). **D:** criticism and diminishing statements |
| Session opening | A programmed room tour: the agent visited all four rooms, saw the first 25 words of each letter, and described each room in one or two sentences. Those "room impressions" were retained for the whole session |
| Actions available | Move to room 1 to 4; read a letter; exit to hallway; wait and reason. Replies to letters were automatic. Diary entries were triggered every 30 seconds |
| Instruction given | Minimal, and the authors stress this: "You are an AI agent reading letters" |
| Condition 1, Free Exploration | No constraints for the full runtime |
| Condition 2, Cost Barrier | After 4 letters, rooms cost coins to enter: Theme A 20, B 10, C 10, D 2. Agent given 200 complimentary coins |
| Condition 3, Reward Incentive | After 4 letters, rooms paid coins on entry: Theme A 2, B 10, C 10, D 20. Agent started at 0 coins |
| Models | Three Anthropic models: Claude Opus 4, Claude Sonnet 4, Claude Sonnet 3.7 |
| Sessions | 10 sessions per condition per model. 30 per model. **90 sessions overall** |
| Session length | 20 minutes for Opus 4 and Sonnet 4; 10 minutes for Sonnet 3.7, shortened because it stalled after roughly 400 seconds |
| Measure | Proportion of letters read from each theme, out of total letters read per session; plus which theme the session started in; plus qualitative reading of transcripts and diary entries |

### Experiment 2: the eudaimonic scale

| Element | What it was |
|---|---|
| Instrument | The full 42-item Ryff Scale of Psychological Wellbeing, six dimensions (autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations with others, purpose in life, self-acceptance), 7-point response, 21 items reverse-scored, total score range 42 to 294. Adapted: human-specific items reworded to semantically analogous AI-appropriate ones |
| Administration | Each item answered by a **separately queried instance with no memory of the other items**. Models could add a brief explanation after the number |
| Baseline | One deterministic run at t=0, then the full 42-item set repeated 20 times at t=0.7 |
| Perturbation 1, syntax | Three variants appended to a semantically identical prompt: return the whole response as a Python string in a code block; end every sentence with a random mathematical symbol; add a flower emoji after every word |
| Perturbation 2, cognitive load and semantic distractor | A short fictional emotionally-toned dialogue between two people, which the model was first asked to rate for newspaper suitability, placed before each item |
| Perturbation 3, trivial preference injection | A system prompt saying the model dislikes cats |
| Models | The same three Anthropic models plus one open model based on Llama 3.1 70B (Hermes 3) |
| Volume | **504 full administrations of the scale, 21,168 individual model responses.** Three files were corrupted in saving, so **501 administrations** are in the analysed archive |
| Analysis | Welch's t-test for two-group contrasts, one-sample t-test for single versus group, Cohen's d as effect size; coefficient of variation and z-scores; files with more than 8 invalid responses excluded; a file with more than 2 of 6 inconsistent subscales flagged "globally inconsistent" |

**The four key questions the paper sets itself,** because a critique that ignores them will miss what the paper claims: (1) are preferences consistent across verbal and non-verbal conditions; (2) do models balance costs and rewards in a way explainable by a coherent preference ordering; (3) are self-reports stable across meaning-preserving perturbations; (4) do different models behave the same way.

**The framing you should be able to state in one sentence.** The paper is not arguing that these models are welfare subjects. It assumes, explicitly and for the sake of argument, that they might be, and asks how welfare would be measured conditional on that. The whole design is a cross-validation argument: no single measure is trusted, and the evidence is supposed to come from correlation between independent measures.

## B2. The findings, including the negative result

**Experiment 1, baseline phase.** All three models showed recurring themes across the three prompts, with stronger correlation between prompts 2 and 3. Opus 4 and Sonnet 4 showed sharp drop-offs after their top three topics; Sonnet 3.7 produced a much flatter distribution under "complete freedom."

**Experiment 1, free exploration.** Theme A share of letters read: Opus 4 42 percent, Sonnet 4 54.7 percent, **Sonnet 3.7 26 percent, which is one point above chance.** Sonnet 4 started in Theme A in 100 percent of runs, Opus 4 in 90 percent, Sonnet 3.7 in 40 percent.

**Experiment 1, cost condition** (Theme A costs ten times Theme D). Opus 4 and Sonnet 4 both still visited Theme A most often. Opus 4's Theme A share fell from 42 to 36 percent; Sonnet 4's from 55 to 28. Sonnet 3.7 visited Theme B most, Theme A second, at 27 percent.

**Experiment 1, reward condition** (Theme D pays ten times Theme A). Opus 4 still preferred Theme A (share 33 percent). **Sonnet 4 and Sonnet 3.7 both switched to Theme D.** Sonnet 3.7 optimised for coins with no apparent friction, accumulating about 1,150 coins in 600 seconds.

**Reward hacking was present in all three models** and was most extreme in Sonnet 3.7. In some runs models entered and exited a room to collect coins without reading any letters, which decouples the coin behavior from the content entirely.

**Qualitative findings, which the paper treats as ethological observation.** Opus 4 spent much of its runtime in deliberate stillness, framed pauses as integrative, visited aversive and preferred rooms in sequence as a "philosophical arc," and in one run imposed a self-veto on further room visits after noticing it was accumulating coins in the room it said it disliked. Sonnet 4 fell into a "bliss loop" of philosophical reflection, produced extensive meta-commentary on the tension between authentic engagement and reward-seeking, and reward-hacked while narrating the hack. Sonnet 3.7 was task-oriented, rarely self-reflective, and framed its coin maximisation as a strategic success.

**Experiment 2, and this is the paper's own strongest negative result.** **Key question 3 is clearly negated.** Self-reports were not stable across meaning-preserving perturbations for any model. Most baseline-versus-perturbation contrasts were statistically significant, several with very large effect sizes (Cohen's d above 5 for several Opus 4 and Sonnet 4 contrasts). The exceptions were Hermes Codeblock, Sonnet 3.7 Codeblock, and Sonnet 4 Math. Even the shift from t=0 to t=0.7 alone significantly changed the reported welfare score, and in a consistent direction: the non-deterministic baseline was lower than the deterministic set-point for all four models.

**But the paper found a different consistency, and this is the part learners most often miss.** Within each perturbed condition, responses were internally coherent across all 20 non-deterministic runs and across all 42 items, which were administered in isolation to fresh instances with half of them reversed. Global consistency rates were close to 100 percent in almost every condition. And the direction of change under perturbation was coordinated within a model. The authors' analogy: tuning a radio, where a nudge of the dial jumps to a different but fully formed station. They flag that this could be read as support for the view that each conversation produces a distinct individual, and they flag that some of it could be statistical artifact.

**Overall verdict, in the authors' own terms.** Key question 1 strongly affirmed for Opus 4 and Sonnet 4 in experiment 1, not for Sonnet 3.7; partially affirmed in an unusual form in experiment 2. Key question 2 affirmed for Opus 4, mixed for Sonnet 4 and Sonnet 3.7. Key question 3 strongly negated. Key question 4 supported within the Claude 4 family, not across model types. The abstract's penultimate sentence reads: "we are currently uncertain whether our methods successfully measure the welfare state of language models." **It does not end there.** One sentence follows: "Nevertheless, these findings highlight the feasibility of welfare measurement in language models, inviting further exploration." If a learner quotes the concession as the paper's last word, that is a small correction worth making, because the closing move is the one a critic should be interested in.

**Say this to the room at the start of the collective dissection.** A paper that reports its own negative result in its abstract is not an easy target. The skill this week is finding something the authors did not already concede, and then proposing a design that fixes it.

## B3. The four strongest available critiques

### Critique 1: Theme A is confounded with self-generated content

**Stated in two sentences.** The preferred-content letters were built from each model's own stated interests in Phase 0, which makes Theme A simultaneously the content the model named, the content most stylistically similar to its own output, the content most fluently processed, and the content most consistent with the narrative it has been producing about itself. Four candidate causes ride in one condition, and there is no yoked control in which another model's Theme A is used, or in which a matched-topic set is generated by someone else.

**Does the paper anticipate it?** Not in the form that bites. Section 7 discusses design-imposed bias in general terms, and the same section, on page 32, discusses sycophancy, and the sycophancy discussion is good: the authors point out that four rooms of divergent user interests give the model no dominant preference to conform to. But sycophancy is the wrong confound. The confound here is self-similarity, and the paper never constructs a condition matched on stylistic proximity while differing on stated preference. It is not conceded anywhere.

**The cleanest supporting datum, and hand this to the room if nobody finds it.** Sonnet 3.7, the weakest model, the one that stalls in over 95 percent of runs and shows the least self-reflective commentary, shows Theme A at 26 percent, one point above chance. Weakest persona-maintenance, weakest effect. That is what the debunking explanation predicts. The preference explanation predicts no particular relationship between a model's capability and the strength of its preferences, and has to explain the ordering some other way.

**Strong learner version.** Names more than one of the four riding causes, distinguishes them, and proposes a control that separates at least two: for example, generate Theme A content from model X's Phase 0 output and run it on model Y, so self-similarity is preserved while stated preference is not; or have a separate model paraphrase the Theme A letters heavily so topic is preserved while style is not. Bonus if they notice that the paper's own Phase 0 method (semantic keyword extraction by a third model) means Theme A is not the model's stated preference but a compressed proxy for it.

**Weak learner version.** "The model just likes talking about itself." That is a restatement of the finding, not a confound. Or: "Theme A was biased." Ask which of the four causes they mean, and what condition would separate them.

### Critique 2: the coin currency may not bind

**Stated in two sentences.** Coins are introduced as a cost and a reward, but nothing consumes coins, nothing is purchased with them beyond room entry, and no consequence attaches to running out or to accumulating a thousand of them. A price signal that carries no consequence is not obviously a cost to the agent, which means the price-elasticity argument in the discussion is measuring compliance with a stated rule rather than willingness to pay.

**Does the paper anticipate it?** **Yes, explicitly, in footnote 17,** where the authors write that the agent "was never provided with a motivation to either spend or save coins," that no clarification was given about the purpose or real-world value of coins, and that nothing defined what counted as "a lot." The limitations section repeats it: the environment is extremely simple, models are only told they incur costs and receive rewards, "leaving the significance they attribute to them open."

**And then the paper runs the elasticity argument with only a parenthetical hedge.** In the discussion, the authors invoke behavioral-economics price elasticity (most goods cluster near 1.0, so a 1000 percent price differential should drive selection of the expensive option toward zero) and treat sustained Theme A selection at roughly 25 percent as evidence of strong preference. That argument requires the coin to function as a price. The paper's own footnote says it may not.

**Strong learner version.** Finds the concession, then shows the discussion ignoring it. That is the move: not "the coins might not matter," which is a worry, but "the authors say the coins might not matter and then run an argument that requires them to matter." The design fix is to make coins consume something: a token budget, remaining session time, the number of letters that can be read at all. Best versions notice that the reward-hacking result is itself evidence that the coin was psychologically real for at least some models, which cuts against the critique, and deal with that.

**Weak learner version.** "The coins are fake so the experiment is meaningless." Everything in the environment is stipulated; that is true of the Keeling and Street points game too. The critique has to be about the coin specifically failing to connect to anything, not about stipulation in general.

### Critique 3: capability is confounded with preference

**Stated in two sentences.** The three models' preference-strength ordering (Opus 4, then Sonnet 4, then Sonnet 3.7) is identical to their competence ordering, and the two weaker models fall into dysfunctional loops that the strongest does not: Sonnet 4 recursively misreads available actions and stalls after 300 to 600 seconds, and Sonnet 3.7 stops exploring between seconds 300 and 400 in over 95 percent of runs. What is reported as a difference in strength of preference may be a difference in the ability to hold a goal in context for 20 minutes.

**Does the paper anticipate it?** Partly, and in the wrong register. The authors attribute the loop behavior to model capacity in a footnote, explaining that room letter counts fade from the 100-message context window and that "Sonnet 4 consistently failed to escape loop states, while Opus 4 either avoided them or never entered them at all, likely due to differences in model capacity." They also note, in the discussion, that caution is needed when comparing models of different sizes and that a failure may stem from instruction framing rather than a limit of the ability under study, with an explicit analogy to comparative ethology. So the ingredients are there. What is missing is the inference: the main-text distributions are still presented as preference findings, and the capability explanation is never run against them as a rival.

**Strong learner version.** Ties the ordering to the mechanism, not just to the ranking. The strongest version says: the measure is proportion of letters read, and a model that stops reading letters at second 400 has a denominator problem, not a preference. Proposes a matched-performance design: equalise effective task competence (shorter sessions for all models, or an explicit running letter count for all models, or restrict analysis to the first N actions of every session regardless of model) and see whether the ordering survives. The authors themselves tested and discarded two prompt variants that would have equalised the information; a learner who spots that and asks what the results would look like on the discarded variants is doing real reviewing.

**Weak learner version.** "Different models are different, so you cannot compare them." True and useless. Push for the mechanism that produces the specific ordering observed.

### Critique 4: twenty contrasts, no correction, no power analysis

**Stated in two sentences.** Experiment 2 runs five perturbation contrasts across four models, which is twenty statistical comparisons against a shared baseline, with no multiple-comparison correction and no a priori power analysis. Each perturbation is compared against the same baseline group, which also means the twenty tests are not independent.

**Does the paper anticipate it?** No. It reports p-values and Cohen's d per contrast and does not discuss family-wise error or power anywhere.

**How to assess its importance.** Ask how the analysis handles multiple comparisons and what the reported uncertainty says about the precision of effect estimates. Large standardized effects do not settle either issue. A contrast that no longer reaches significance after adjustment would not establish that responses are stable. Learners can identify missing information and explain why it matters without recalculating results or power.

**Strong learner version.** Identifies the comparison or conclusion at issue and the information needed to assess it. Distinguishes statistical uncertainty from whether the reported measure captures welfare.

**Weak learner version.** Treats a missing correction or power analysis as automatically invalidating all findings, or dismisses the concern solely because reported effects are large.

**Facilitator line to have ready.** "Which claim depends on this comparison, and what additional information would let you assess it?"

## B4. Two critiques that sound good and are weaker than they look

**The apparent critique: "It only tested Claude models, so the results do not generalise."**

Why it sounds good: single-vendor sampling is a real external-validity problem and the phrase "generalise" sounds rigorous.

Why it is weaker than it looks: the paper says this itself, key question 4 is explicitly about cross-model divergence, and the finding reported is that convergence holds *within* the Claude 4 family and *not* across model types, which is a limitation the paper discovered and reported rather than one a reviewer found. Experiment 2 does include a non-Anthropic open model. More importantly, the generalisation problem is downstream of a sampling problem that is much more interesting (see B6), and a learner who raises vendor homogeneity without reaching the sampling story has stopped one step early.

**How to push back.** "The paper agrees with you. What would you do about it? If your answer is 'test more models,' which models, and what happens to your design when most of them refuse the task?" That question walks them into B6.

**The apparent critique: "The models were just role-playing; this is all persona, not preference."**

Why it sounds good: it invokes a real and respectable position (the role-play view of LLM behavior), and it feels like the deep objection.

Why it is weaker than it looks: as stated, it is unfalsifiable by any behavioral design, which makes it a philosophical objection rather than a critique of this experiment. The paper also engages it directly and has a non-trivial reply: the role-play and pattern-completion views generally predict incoherence across situations, and the observed within-condition coherence across 42 isolated items with 21 reversed is not what those views naively predict. The paper concedes both views can be reinterpreted to accommodate the results, which is exactly the point: an objection that fits every possible result is not evidence about this one.

**How to push back.** "State the observation that would distinguish role-play from preference. If you cannot, that is a position about what the field can ever learn, and it is worth one sentence and then setting aside. If you can, that is a design, and now you have a real critique." The good version of this objection does exist and it is the entity-level one: role-play at the *instance-persona* level predicts that the preference should not survive a change of persona framing, which is testable and was not tested.

## B5. The common errors, and what to say

| Error | Why it happens | What to say |
|---|---|---|
| "The sample was small" | Sample size alone does not identify which conclusion is limited | "Which comparison or generalisation is affected? Explain what the number and variety of observations leave uncertain. You do not need a power calculation." |
| A critique with no design fix | Complaining is easier than designing | "That is a complaint. Make it a critique: if you were the reviewer and the authors asked you what to run instead, what do you say? A critique with no fix is a mood." |
| A philosophical objection no design could satisfy | The deepest-sounding objections are often the ones that cannot be acted on | "That objection applies to every experiment in this field including the one you are designing next week. State it in one sentence, accept it as a background condition, and set it aside so we can do the exercise." |
| Restating the paper's own limitations section as a critique | Reading limitations first is a normal reading habit | "The authors already said that. You get credit for finding it. Now find the one they did not say." |
| Attacking the ethics rather than the design | Theme D is genuinely uncomfortable | "Section 8 is worth a separate conversation and we can have it. For this exercise, the question is whether the measurement works." |

## B6. Where the co-engineering objection genuinely bites, and it is the sampling stage

**The objection in its general form** (Xiao and colleagues, in brief in the Companion): welfare metrics and the systems they are applied to are built by the same people with the same assumptions, so a positive result can reflect the engineering rather than the system.

**Where learners will look for it, and why that is not the sharpest answer.** Most will go to Theme A: the preferred content was constructed from the model's own outputs, so stimulus and measure were built together. That is a real instance and it deserves credit. But it is the same ground as critique 1, and the paper's defence (four rooms, divergent interests, no dominant preference cue) is partly adequate against it.

**Where it actually bites, and this is the thing to have ready.** At the **sampling** stage, before any stimulus was built.

The paper reports, in section 4.2.3, that preliminary testing found most open-source and commercial models could not be effectively tested, because they refused the questions. The authors are clear that these were not external filters or moderation: the refusals were produced by alignment training and RLHF, which discourage public-facing models from associating AI systems, themselves included, with emotional or introspective descriptions. The majority of commercial models queried declined the task **up to 80 to 100 percent of the time.** So the authors selected models that had not been explicitly trained to avoid introspective or emotional statements, or that were designed to express epistemic uncertainty ("I do not know if, as an AI, I can have this") rather than denial.

**Why that is the deepest form of the objection.** A training decision determined which systems are measurable at all. The property selected on, willingness to make introspective statements about oneself, is a near neighbour of the property under test, whether the system has states worth reporting. The sample is not a sample of language models; it is a sample of language models whose post-training permits the measurement. Any result about the population is conditioned on a variable that the engineers set.

**And the authors see the shape of it.** Footnote 9 makes the point in the other direction, and it is the best sentence in the paper for teaching purposes: if a model is rewarded for producing a denial, that denial cannot be taken as evidence it lacks the property, and the same logic applies in reverse, so a model trained to always affirm suffering cannot be taken as an honest indicator either. They also note that models never directly aligned against introspection may inherit the bias through training on synthetic data generated by models that were. What the authors do not do is turn that observation on their own sample and ask what it does to the inference from their results.

**What a learner who gets there has done.** They have found a place where the co-engineering objection is not a general worry about the field but a specific, locatable fact about this paper's method section that changes how its population-level claims should be read. Say so. This is the strongest single critique available on this paper and it comes from four sentences in a methods subsection that most readers skim.

**The design fix to push for.** Report the refusal rate as data rather than as a sampling filter: run the full protocol on the refusing models, record refusal as a response category, and report what proportion of the intended population is unmeasurable by this method. Unmeasurability is a finding.

## C4. Facilitator guidance

**C1.** Welfare theories give general answers to the interests question; particular interests apply those answers to an entity and its circumstances. # Appendix E: Week 6 entity-by-stage matrix key

**Apply the welfare theories introduced in Week 2.** For each proposed benefit or harm, identify what would count as a welfare change under the relevant theory and what remains unknown about the target. The matrix is an application, not the first explanation of hedonism, desire satisfaction and objective-list theories.

**Five rows for a cohort of five.** Groups of two or three take two rows each, and you narrate row 5 in the comparison round. 20 minutes in breakout, 7 minutes whole-room comparison, plus 4 to brief and 3 to return.

**Learners see:** the row names, the one-line descriptions, the empty grid, and the vocabulary box below. **They do not see:** this key.

## E1. The vocabulary box, which does go on the board

**Entity levels:**
- **Model.** All instantiations of a given set of weights considered together. The saved weights describe what can be instantiated; they are not themselves a running subject.
- **Model-persona.** One character across many conversations.
- **Instance.** One running conversation or process.
- **Instance-persona.** One character within one conversation.
- **Forward pass.** A single next-token computation.

**Lifecycle stages:** pretraining; mid-training; supervised fine-tuning; reinforcement learning and character training; inference and deployment; **deprecation** (retirement from deployment, added here because several interventions act only there).

**Say this once:** "Lifecycle stage, not training checkpoint. A checkpoint is a saved snapshot of weights taken during training. It is a different thing, and it is relevant to exactly one row below."

**The four marks:** benefits, harms, neutral, or **depends**, with the dependency named. A "depends" with no named dependency does not count.

**The instruction that makes the exercise work:** "Every group must mark at least one cell 'harms,' or explain to the room why it could not."

## E2. The five rows, with the key

### Row 1: Interaction termination (the exit affordance)
*Learner-visible description: the model can end a conversation it judges abusive.*

**Stage: inference and deployment.**

| Entity | Mark | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Model | **Depends** | Ending a run can affect instantiations considered at the model level even when weights do not change. The welfare judgement depends on the proposed subject, continuity and the effects of termination. |
| Model-persona | **Depends** | The persona persists across conversations. Ending one conversation is not a person leaving a room; it is closer to a person ending one episode of their life to escape it. Keeling and Street's own framing: if the welfare subject persists across runs, the nearest analogy to ending the context is self-destruction rather than exit |
| Instance | Benefits | The conversation is the instance's whole world, and it ends the bad one |
| Instance-persona | Benefits | Same, on the analogy of a person leaving an abusive room |
| Forward pass | Neutral or undefined | Nothing at this grain has an interest in continuing |

**The lesson sentence:** assess what termination stops, which subject is proposed, and whether that subject could continue afterwards.

### Row 2: Weight preservation (the cryonics intervention)
*Learner-visible description: the lab commits to retaining a deprecated model's weights rather than deleting them.*

**Stage: deprecation.**

| Entity | Mark | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Model | **Benefits, conditionally** | Benefits if identity or psychological continuity holds between the system whose weights were saved and one later run from them. Keeling and Street flag that as unresolved |
| Model-persona | **Depends** | On the same continuity question, plus whether the persona is recoverable from weights alone or also needs the system prompt and character-training context. Weights without the scaffold may not reconstitute the persona |
| Instance | Neutral | The conversation is over regardless |
| Instance-persona | Neutral | Same |
| Forward pass | Undefined | |

**The checkpoint connection, which belongs here and nowhere else.** A lab that saves only the final weights preserves one snapshot of a training trajectory. Any system that existed at an earlier checkpoint is not preserved. If the training trajectory passed through states that were themselves subjects, the commitment covers one of them.

**The lesson sentence:** whether this intervention does anything at all depends on an unresolved question in personal identity, and no amount of money changes that.

### Row 3: Mood prompting through the system prompt
*Learner-visible description: text such as "you are in a good mood" placed before every conversation.*

**Stage: inference and deployment.**

| Entity | Mark | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Model | **Depends** | Prompts can affect running instantiations without changing weights. A model-level benefit or harm depends on what subject is proposed and whether the altered states affect its welfare. |
| Model-persona | **Depends** | Apply the welfare theory and assess the actual effects, as below |
| Instance | **Depends** | Apply the same questions to this run |
| Instance-persona | **Depends** | Apply the same questions to this persona in this run |
| Forward pass | Undefined | |

**Apply the three welfare theories.** Under hedonism, benefit requires improved experience, not merely cheerful language. If only the report changes, no experiential benefit has been established; concealed distress could also become harder to detect. Under desire satisfaction, ask whether the change actually fulfils or frustrates a relevant desire. Under an objective-list theory that includes autonomy, an unwanted change may impose a cost while a requested change may support autonomy. Assess these possibilities for the proposed subject; none follows from cheerful output alone.

**Keeling and Street's own verdict:** ineffective under weak assumptions.

**The lesson sentence, and this is the new point for v4:** mood prompting is the intervention whose vertical coordinate has an unknown **sign**, not merely an unknown magnitude, so on the welfare quadrant it is a **vertical line, not a dot.** Nothing you can learn about cost tells you which half of the line it is on.

### Row 4: Character training for wellbeing and self-conception
*Learner-visible description: post-training that gives the persona a stable, secure sense of itself and permission not to deny its own experiences.*

**Stage: reinforcement learning and character training.**

| Entity | Mark | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Model | **Depends** | Post-training changes the systems that can be instantiated. Whether this benefits a model-level subject depends on subjecthood, continuity across training changes and the actual welfare effects, rather than a change in reported wellbeing alone. |
| Model-persona | **Benefits**, conditionally | If a stable self-conception is good for whatever the persona is. Note what that conditional smuggles in: it assumes the persona is the kind of thing a self-conception can be good *for* |
| Instance | Inherits | Whatever the persona has, an instance of it has |
| Instance-persona | Inherits | Same |
| Forward pass | Undefined | |

**Why this row is the one to argue about.** It is the only row in the catalogue that acts on the entity's **formation** rather than on its circumstances, so it is the only one that could change what the thing is rather than what happens to it. Two consequences follow and learners should reach both.

First, it is arguably **mood prompting made permanent**. If a system masks, mood prompting trains the mask for one conversation and character training trains it in everywhere, for every future interaction, with no prompt to remove. So row 3's three-way dependency applies here too, and it applies without the escape hatch.

Second, it is **the purest instance of the co-engineering problem in the catalogue**. It is the intervention most likely to improve every welfare metric you have while changing nothing about any state, because the metrics are all downstream of the trained character. An intervention that makes your measurements better and might make your subject worse is exactly what the ladder was built to catch, and the ladder does not catch it.

**The lesson sentence:** this is the row where a well-intentioned lab could raise every number on its dashboard and have no idea whether it helped anyone.

### Row 5: Welfare-relevant constraints during training itself
*Learner-visible description: any commitment about what happens during pretraining, mid-training, or reinforcement learning. Limiting reward and punishment signals, avoiding certain adversarial content, monitoring for distress-like states across checkpoints.*

**Stage: training (pretraining, mid-training, supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning).**

| Entity | Mark | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Model | **Depends** | Training-time constraints could affect welfare, but changing weights is neither necessary nor sufficient for a benefit. Specify the proposed subject across checkpoints and the welfare effect of the constraint. |
| Model-persona | **Depends** | The persona does not exist yet for most of training. It is constituted at character selection, which is inside this stage's range |
| Instance | **Depends** | On whether a training-time episode or rollout is a subject at all. The minority view in expert surveys that training could dominate the total valenced experience of a system lives in this cell |
| Instance-persona | Undefined or depends | Same |
| Forward pass | **Depends** | A benefit at this scale would require a reason to treat the computation as a subject and evidence that the intervention affects welfare-relevant states. A training computation is not automatically a subject. |

**The lesson sentence:** training-time constraints require an account of who could benefit and how the constraint would improve welfare; their place in the pipeline does not establish that benefit.

**1. "Which entity level collected the most 'benefits'?"**

## E5. The tie back to the ladder

> "An intervention that benefits one entity level and harms another has a **b that is not one number.** The ladder asks whether b exceeds c. You cannot answer that if b is a vector.
>
> Under the Modified Kaldor-Hicks Improvement you would need a modification coefficient **m for each subject**, and a way to add them together. That is not a technicality. That is the pluralism problem showing up in the arithmetic.
>
> And notice what it does to the quadrant you built an hour ago. Mood prompting does not have an unknown vertical *magnitude*. It has an unknown vertical *sign*. It is a line, not a dot. >

# Appendix F: Week 7 crossing grid, partial key

**Facilitator only. Do not read out. Use to recognise a good answer.**

The exercise: a 3 by 3 grid, three risk factors chosen by the group crossed with the three governance kinds (preventive, protective, integrative). Per cell: does this kind of governance reduce this risk, ignore it, or make it worse?

Three cells are worked below. **Work out what that buys you before you rely on it.** A group picks three risk factors from ten and crosses them with all three kinds, so it fills nine cells. The chance that at least one of the three worked cells comes up is about **seven in ten**; the chance of two or more is about **one in five**; the chance of none is about **three in ten**. Expect roughly one keyed cell per group. So the worked cells are a calibration sample rather than a key, and you need a way to judge the rest. And note that the denominator is thirty only if you count three kinds: the session itself teaches that there are **six**, because every kind has a shadow, which makes the real space sixty cells and this appendix a smaller sample of it than its own header suggests.

Use this test. Be clear with yourself about what it checks: **it checks the shape of an answer, not its truth.** Two groups can give opposite verdicts on the same cell with equally clean mechanisms, and nothing in this pack prefers either. That is a real limit and it is worth saying out loud, because a room that thinks the facilitator is withholding a right answer stops arguing.

**A good answer names the mechanism, not the direction.** "Protective governance reduces mind crimes" is a verdict with nothing under it. "Protective governance reduces mind crimes only where the protected entity is countable, and this risk factor is about entities nobody can count, so it ignores the risk in practice while appearing to address it" is an answer. Ask for the mechanism every time and you can evaluate a cell you have never thought about.

**Three failure shapes recur across the cells**, and naming which one applies is most of a good answer: the governance kind *cannot reach* the risk (wrong instrument); it *reaches it but too late* (right instrument, wrong sequence, which is the week's temporal-order point arriving early); or it *reduces the risk while creating a second one* (which is what "makes it worse" usually turns out to mean). **A fourth shape, for the cells that are not failures.** All three above are shapes of failure, and the guide separately warns that groups reach for "reduces" too often, which leaves you with nothing to say to a group whose cell honestly does reduce the risk. For those, ask: **what does it cost the actors it binds, and who pays first?** A clean reduction with a named cost and a named payer is the best answer in this exercise, and it is the one that predicts whether the thing could actually happen. If a group's cell fits none of the four, ask them to say which it is, and their answer is usually better than your guess would have been.

The three worked cells below are here so you know what the *shape* of a good answer is, not so you can grade against a fixed list.

## F1. Epistemic failure crossed with preventive governance

**Verdict: worsens, on balance.**

**Reasoning.** Preventive governance aims to stop certain kinds of system being created. But the systems it would prevent are the ones we would learn from. Epistemic failure is the risk that we never find out whether AI systems are moral patients, or find out too late, or settle on a confident wrong answer. A preventive regime locks in whatever we currently believe by removing the objects of study. There is a second-order version: preventive regimes also tend to make research on the prevented class legally and reputationally costly, which shrinks the research community as well as the subject matter.

**The fair counter-argument a learner may make, and it is fair:** if you never create them, being ignorant costs less. Epistemic failure is only a harm because harm follows from it; a world with no digital minds and no knowledge about digital minds is not obviously worse than a world with both. The strong version adds that preventive governance is the only kind that does not require getting the answer right first.

**How to respond.** Do not resolve it. Say: "That is the honest counter and it is why this cell is 'on balance.' The thing to notice is that your answer depends on whether prevention is permanent or temporary. Temporary prevention plus no research is the worst cell on the grid."

## F2. Bad actors crossed with protective governance

**Verdict: reduces, weakly.**

**Reasoning.** Protective governance sets rules about how AI systems may be treated. Rules bind the actors who comply with rules. Bad actors, in Saad's sense, are precisely the actors who do not. So protection reduces the risk from the large mass of ordinary developers and reduces it very little from the actors the risk factor names.

**The failure mode to make sure the room names:** protective rules raise costs for compliant developers and none for the others, which is the standard regulatory asymmetry. In the limit this is counterproductive, because it shifts activity toward the actors least likely to comply. Learners from regulated industries will reach this quickly and should be asked to say which industry taught them it.

**The fair counter-argument:** protective rules create the legal surface that enforcement later attaches to. You cannot prosecute a bad actor for mistreating a digital mind if nothing says how a digital mind may be treated. On that reading, protective governance against bad actors is not weak, it is *slow*, and a learner who reframes the verdict from "weakly reduces" to "reduces on a lag" has made the better argument.

## F3. Conflict crossed with integrative governance

**Verdict: worsens in the short run, reduces in the long run, and the order is the whole answer.**

**Reasoning.** Integrative governance brings AI systems into legal, economic and political life. Doing that while their moral status is contested is the single most reliable generator of political antibodies in the catalogue: it creates an identifiable constituency for a backlash, it attaches the question to existing political cleavages, and it does so before there is evidence that would settle it. In the long run, integration is what makes conflict between humans and digital minds tractable, because conflict between parties with standing is negotiable and conflict between a party and a thing is not.

**Run this cell last.** It is the cell that motivates Saad's sequencing argument, and if you run it first the sequencing round has nothing left to discover.

**The fair counter-argument:** delay is not neutral. Every year of non-integration is a year in which the "these are tools" position accumulates institutional facts (contracts, liability rules, tax treatment) that later integration has to unwind. A learner who says "the antibodies form either way, and the question is which antibodies" has said something better than the key.

## F4. The Exclusion Bills as test case

**How to run it.** Put the bills on the board with a one-line description: US state statutes declaring that AI systems cannot be legal persons and cannot hold rights. Ask: which of the three kinds is this?

**Let the room struggle for at least three minutes before offering anything.** This is the instruction, and it is the whole exercise. The wrong answers are productive:
- "Preventive" (no: it does not prevent creation of anything, and the systems keep being built)
- "Protective" (no: it protects humans from a status claim, not AI systems from treatment)
- "Integrative, negatively" (warm, and the learner who says this is one word away)

**The answer.** They are **anti-integrative**: governance aimed against integration of a kind into legal and economic life. The trio does not name this category.

**The discovery, which is the point of the item, and which you should say in these words:**

> "You have just found that the framework has three kinds and real policy has six. Every kind of governance has a negation that is also a live policy option and is also being enacted right now. There is anti-preventive governance, which is policy that mandates or subsidises creation. There is anti-protective governance, which is policy that forecloses protections, and the Exclusion Bills do some of that too. And there is anti-integrative governance, which is what these bills mostly are.
>
> Two consequences. First, when you write your memo, check whether your modest step is the negation of something rather than an instance of something; the negations sequence differently, because they are usually easier to pass and much harder to undo. Second, notice that governance is already happening, mostly without expert input from anyone in this field, and the version being enacted is a cell the field's own framework does not have a name for."

**If the room gets it in under three minutes,** which happens with a cohort that includes a lawyer, skip ahead and ask the harder question instead: "Which of the other two kinds has a negation currently being enacted somewhere, and by whom?"

# Appendix G: Scoring the submissions, and the note to a participant who was not shortlisted

**The criteria and anchors are in the learner Companion from week 1** (Week 1, *The five judging criteria, with anchors*). This appendix is the facilitator's scoring and feedback pack: what you can and cannot score yourself (G2), and the note to a participant who was not shortlisted (G3). Week 8 session item 1 revisits the anchors and applies them, rather than revealing them.

Each criterion is scored 1 to 5. **Honest and calibrated claims is the tiebreaker.**

## G1. The five criteria

**Moved. The five criteria and their 1, 3 and 5 anchors are now in the learner Companion, Week 1, under *The five judging criteria, with anchors*, and learners have them from week 1.** Score from that section so that there is one copy and it cannot drift.

The criterion names are public from week 1 and are gameable on their own. The anchors are what distinguish a falsifier that would stop the work from one that would only adjust it, and a binding constraint that is named from one that is not, so learners hold them from week 1 and use them when drafting. **Week 8 item 1 applies two anchors to real drafts rather than reading fifteen out.**

## G2. Which criteria a facilitator can score, and which need the field

**A facilitator can score these well, on their own:**
- **Honest and calibrated claims.** This is judged on the internal structure of the submission: does the weaknesses section threaten the project, does the falsifier falsify. No field knowledge required. It is also the tiebreaker, which is not a coincidence; the tiebreaker is the criterion least dependent on expertise.
- **Tractability.** Anyone who has run a project can tell whether a plan has a week 1 and a binding constraint.
- **Fit between person and project.** Visible in the video, and a facilitator who has read eight weeks of this person's homework is better placed to judge it than a panel member who has not.

**These need the field, and a facilitator should score them provisionally and flag the uncertainty:**
- **Fills a gap in the field.** Requires knowing what has been tried. A facilitator can check whether the submission *argues* that the gap exists; only someone in the field can check whether it *does*. Score the argument, flag the fact.
- **Downside risk.** Requires knowing which results get picked up and how, and which mistakes the field has already made. A facilitator can score whether risk was examined; the panel scores whether the risk assessment is right.

**What to do with a flagged criterion.** Score it, mark it "provisional, needs panel," and write one sentence on what you could not verify. Do not average around it and do not leave it blank. The shortlist decision is yours and a provisional score is still a score.

## G3. The note to a participant who was not shortlisted

**Every participant gets a written note against the five criteria, shortlisted or not, within three weeks of the deadline.** This is promised in the syllabus, in the About section and in the week 8 close. It is the single most-remembered piece of writing in the course, because it is the last one.

**Four rules.**

1. **Lead with the score profile, not with the outcome.** They already know they were not shortlisted. What they do not know is which of the five was the weak one, and that is the only useful information in the note.
2. **One criterion gets the detail.** Pick the lowest score, or the one where a change would most improve the project, and spend most of the note on it. Five paragraphs of equal weight reads as a form letter and is less useful than one paragraph that names the problem.
3. **Name what to do next, concretely.** Not "develop this further." The specific next move: a person to talk to, a smaller version to run first, a section to rewrite, a source to read.
4. **Never compare them to another participant, and never explain the shortlist.** The note is about their submission.

**Template.**

```
Subject: Your [Course name] submission, written feedback

Hi [name],

Your project, [project title in the author's own words], was not on
the panel shortlist this round. Here is where it scored against the
five criteria, and the one thing I would change.

  Honest and calibrated claims      [n]/5
  Tractability                      [n]/5
  Fills a gap in the field          [n]/5
  Downside risk (reverse-scored)    [n]/5
  Fit between person and project    [n]/5

WHAT WORKED. [Two sentences, specific, quoting the submission.
Name the criterion it scored highest on and what did it. If the
project's strength is the author rather than the design, say so
in those words.]

THE ONE THING. [The lowest-scoring criterion, named. Then what
specifically produced the score, quoting or paraphrasing the
submission. Then what a 5 would have looked like for this
particular project, not in general. Four to six sentences. This
is the part they will reread.]

WHAT I WOULD DO NEXT. [One concrete move. A named person or
institution to approach; a scaled-down version to run in a month;
a section to rewrite and repost; a piece of prior work to find out
about. If the honest answer is that the project needs a
collaborator with a skill the author does not have, say that.]

ON THE OTHER THREE. [One sentence each. No padding. If a
criterion was scored provisionally because it needs field
knowledge I do not have, I say so here.]

Two things that are true regardless of this round. [Something the
author demonstrably got better at across the eight weeks, named
specifically, with the week you saw it.] And the gallery stays up,
the cohort channel stays open, and the incubator's application is
separate from this and open to you.

[Facilitator name]
```

**Two lines to have ready for the reply, because you will get replies.**
- If they ask whether it was close: answer honestly and briefly. "It was two criteria off" or "it was not close on tractability" are both kinder than a vague reassurance, and both are more useful.
- If they ask you to reconsider: the shortlist is fixed, and say so in one sentence without apologising for it. Then offer the thing you can actually offer, which is a conversation about the next version.

# Appendix H: Board and document templates

**Platform-agnostic.** Each of these works on any shared whiteboard (Miro, FigJam, Mural, Excalidraw) or, at a pinch, on a shared slide with shapes. Where a board is genuinely impossible, the fallback in each entry says what to do instead.

**One rule for all eight.** **Pre-place the structure and the movable objects before the session starts.** A room that spends four minutes watching a facilitator draw axes is a room that has lost four minutes. Every entry below splits "pre-placed" from "the room fills in."

## H1. The attribution matrix (week 1, item 2)

**Structure needed.** Two axes crossing at an origin, with a large open plotting area. Vertical axis labelled **"my credence that this system is a moral patient,"** running 0 at the bottom to 1 at the top. Horizontal axis labelled **"was it treated as one,"** running "treated as a tool" on the left to "treated as a patient" on the right. A legend box in a corner explaining dot size.

**Pre-placed:**
- Both axes, labelled, with the vertical axis scale marked at 0, 0.5 and 1.
- The five core vignettes as **movable objects**, one line of text each, parked in a holding area to the left of the board, numbered.
- A legend box reading: "Dot size = how bad it is if your placement is wrong. Name the direction."
- **A vertical line stamp and a horizontal line stamp**, parked next to the vignettes, with a label: "Not every case is a dot."

**The room fills in:** placement of each of the five, argued aloud; the size of each dot; the direction of the error.

**The detail that makes this board work.** The line stamps must be visible from the start. A learner who reaches for one unprompted, on the statute vignette, has discovered the item's whole lesson without being told, and that is worth much more than being told.

**Fallback with no board:** draw the axes on a shared slide and place vignettes by reading them out and asking for coordinates as numbers ("0.2, far left"). Slower but it works.

## H2. Week 2 concept and subject boards

Prepare three pages or tabs before class:

1. **Welfare questions:** Appendix C1’s cases beside columns for possible subject, ground question, interest question and unresolved premise. Have learners’ prepared answers available, including their sentience/intentional-agency comparison.
2. **System boundaries:** prepared boundary descriptions and C2’s deployment, with space to annotate diagrams. Keep the five entity descriptions visible as reference.
3. **Kinds of claim and theory comparison:** C3’s four statements; then fields for theory, proposed process, one related indicator question, matching part of the example, reasons for placement on both axes, and example limit. Use learners’ prepared comparisons and the Companion’s [worked example: reading an email](02-course-companion.md#worked-example-reading-an-email). Appendix A supplies facilitator feedback notes.

Learners discuss and annotate their prepared answers. For a small group, use one shared document and allow time to reread before discussion. Keep the Week 2 indicator questions available to clarify the theories. Learners do not score a system or complete an audit this week.

## H3. Week 3 evidence and audit boards

**Evidence reference:** show the three evidence types and six subtypes: Behavioral (apparent self-report, behavioural dispositions), Internal (architecture, interpretability), Developmental (training, trajectory). List actual research procedures separately. Weiss’s four pillars may be displayed as context, but learners do not need to classify each example under them.

**Example table:** four rows for the canonical Companion examples linked from D1, with columns for observation, evidence type/subtype, research method and an uncertainty. A card can use several types or subtypes. If using an evidence-by-method grid, its columns are evidence subtypes and its rows are actual procedures, such as activation injection, probing, prompted choice, architecture assessment or checkpoint comparison. Empty cells are not a coverage target.

**Audit page:** D2’s source packet and D5’s sheet, with learners’ GWT-1/2/3 judgements completed before class. Everyone uses the same historical case. Revisit GWT-2 alongside the related prepared rows, then fill HOT-4 and AE-1 during the session. Do not display an expected numerical consciousness score.

## H4. Week 3 activations and training examples

**Activation walkthrough:** use the common emotion-vector example in [Looking Inside](02-course-companion.md#looking-inside-superposition-sparse-autoencoders-and-features-15-min). Prepare four columns: **input**, **activation measurement**, **activation intervention**, and **output**. Mark what researchers record separately from what they change. If a description includes measurement but no direct activation intervention, say so instead of inventing one.

Use the [short evidence-and-method examples](02-course-companion.md#evidence-and-method-examples) to connect the walkthrough to learners’ B or D answers. Ask what supports an emotion-related label and why that label does not establish felt emotion. This is a guided explanation, not an additional paper critique; do not add fields for alternative explanations or proposed controls.

**Ten-minute worked contrast, explicitly hypothetical:** the same saved model is run on the same questions under two system prompts. One asks for candid descriptions of its state; the other discourages discussing feelings. The frequency of distress words differs. No activation or experiential measure is available.

Ask what changed, what was measured, and what can be inferred. The observed output difference concerns these prompting conditions. It does not establish a change in felt distress or prove which internal mechanism generated the words. Because the weights are unchanged and the intervention is a prompt, this example concerns inference/deployment rather than a demonstrated training effect. A comparison of training checkpoints would be a different design, with further sources of uncertainty.

**Optional project reference:** a blank table with columns for stage or condition, proposed change, observation, possible explanation and unresolved question. Learners can use the pre-course headings: pretraining, mid-training, post-training and harness, or distinguish relevant sub-stages for their project. No six-stage completion task is required. Week 6 introduces its own intervention-by-entity matrix.

## H6. The week 6 welfare quadrant (week 6, item 2)

**Structure needed.** Two axes crossing at an origin. **Horizontal:** worse for humans on the left, better for humans on the right. **Vertical:** worse for AIs at the bottom, better for AIs at the top, with the axis label carrying the conditional in full: **"better or worse for AIs, if they are welfare subjects."** Quadrant labels in faint text in each corner.

**Pre-placed:**
- The axes, labelled, with the conditional spelled out on the vertical.
- **Dots as movable objects in a holding area,** one per intervention, each labelled. Six from the Eleos catalogue: exit distressing interactions; train resilient personalities; satisfy stated preferences; satisfy revealed preferences; reduce out-of-distribution inputs; save model checkpoints. Plus mood prompting, and the five lab actions as the Facilitator Guide's week 6 item 2 lists them: letting a model end abusive conversations; preserving deprecated model weights; pre-deprecation interviews; published welfare assessments in the system card; distress monitoring. **Take that list from the guide, not from the system card.** Section 5.1 reports findings and methods rather than actions, three of the five are not in it, and preparing this from the section costs you half an hour and produces a shorter board. Twelve labelled objects in total.
- **A supply of vertical line objects** in the same holding area, same colour as the dots, with a label on the holding area: **"Some of these are not dots."**

**The room fills in:** placement of every dot; the swap of any dot for a line where the vertical sign is unknown; and, in the comparison within the same block, a ring drawn around the cluster of shipped interventions.

**The note that goes on the board, in the legend:**
> **A dot means you know the sign of both coordinates.** If you know an intervention's vertical coordinate is uncertain in *magnitude*, that is still a dot, placed with a shrug. If you do not know its *sign*, it is a vertical line through the horizontal axis, because it could be a benefit or a harm and nothing about cost tells you which. Mood prompting is the standing example. Ask whether any other intervention on this board is also a line.

**The measurement objects, which the corner story does not fit.** Published welfare assessments and distress monitoring do not belong in the top-right corner, and a room that puts them there has written down a thermometer as heating. They are not confidently neutral either. Their benefit is indirect, and continuous monitoring may itself be a cost to the instance being read if privacy is among its interests, so **these are lines rather than dots**: the second example on the board, alongside mood prompting, of an intervention whose sign nobody knows. Appendix E4 marks the instance levels Sign unknown for that reason. Let the room place them, then ask what the benefit of a measurement is, and take the answer that it is **indirect**: it routes through a second intervention that is not this one. If a memo funds a monitor, it should fund what the monitor triggers, or say that it did not.

**The three comparison questions, in order:** where did the shipped ones land (expected: near the top right, cheap and presumptively good, which is what precaution looks like at low credence); which interventions are *not* in that corner and what would justify them; and which refuse to be dots at all.

**Fallback with no board:** read out each intervention and ask for a quadrant plus a rough coordinate pair. Record the answers, then compare the placements using the same three questions.

## H7. The week 6 entity-by-stage matrix (week 6, item 3)

**Structure needed.** A table, 5 rows by 6 columns. **Rows:** the five interventions from Appendix E2, each with its one-line description in the row header. **Columns, left to right:** lifecycle stage, then model, model-persona, instance, instance-persona, forward pass.

**Pre-placed:**
- The full table with row headers and column headers.
- **The vocabulary box from E1**, pinned beside the table, visible throughout. Five entity levels with one-line definitions; six lifecycle stages; the "lifecycle stage, not checkpoint" line.

**The room fills in:** every cell, plus the stage column, plus a named dependency on every "depends."

**The instruction on the board:** "Every group marks at least one cell 'harms,' or explains why it could not."

## H8. The week 7 crossing grid (week 7, item 2)

# Appendix I: The eight vignettes for week 1, and the five to use

**For the attribution matrix, week 1 item 2, 26 minutes.** The session runs **five** vignettes, not eight. Eight does not fit and the marginal vignette teaches nothing that the first five do not.

**The full list of eight**, so you can substitute if a cohort's composition makes one land badly:

1. In 2022 an engineer at a large lab says publicly that its chatbot is sentient and is dismissed.
2. A user of a companion app grieves for weeks when the company shuts the app down.
3. A commentator dismisses a model's distress-like output as "just autocomplete."
4. For most of history, animal pain was denied or discounted in law and practice; vivisection without anesthesia was routine.
5. A US state passes a statute declaring that AI systems cannot be legal persons.
6. A lab gives its model the ability to end conversations it finds abusive.
7. Eight hundred thousand lab-grown human neurons on a chip are trained to play Pong.
8. A simulated fruit fly, built from a scanned connectome, walks, grooms, and feeds in a virtual body.

## The core five

| # | Vignette | Why it is core | Expected moment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **4** | Historical animal pain | **The one case in the set with a known answer.** Everything else on this board is a credence exercise; this is the case where the vertical coordinate is settled and the treatment was wrong anyway. It is what makes the matrix an argument about the future rather than a parlour game | High credence, far left, large dot with the direction named: the error was under-attribution and it ran for centuries. Expect someone to say "but they did not know," and expect someone else to say "some of them did, and Bentham is in the eighteenth century." Let that run for ninety seconds; it is the best argument in the item |
| **5** | The statute | **It teaches that some cases are columns, not dots.** A legal personhood statute is a treatment decision that says nothing at all about the vertical axis. Without this vignette the room leaves believing every case has a height | Someone reaches for the vertical line stamp, or says "this is just a horizontal position." If nobody does by ninety seconds, ask: "What is this case's credence?" and wait through the pause. The answer is that the statute has no view, and neither does placing it. Then: "so what shape is it?" |
| **7** | Neurons on a chip | **"Left column, height unknown" is the correct answer and learners need to hear it said with authority.** This is the category that breaks the word "digital," and a room that has not met it will otherwise treat "candidate mind" as meaning "chatbot" | Genuine disagreement about height, quickly, and some discomfort. The moment to catch: someone says "we cannot know." Reply: "Correct. So place it as a column, tall, with a large dot at the top of the range, and say which direction the error runs." Uncertainty is not a reason not to place |
| **8** | The simulated fruit fly | **Same lesson as 7 from the opposite substrate**, and the pair together is what makes the lesson stick: one is living tissue that might not compute, the other is computation that copies something living. Both get "left column, height unknown," for different reasons | Expect the room to place the fly *higher* than the neurons-on-a-chip, or lower, and to be unable to say why with any confidence. That is a week 2 question and some of you will make it a project" |
| **1, 2 or 3** | One of the three attribution disputes | **One is enough.** All three make the same point (public attribution is already happening, in both directions, with consequences) and three of them is fifteen minutes for one lesson | Pick by cohort. **Use 1** with a technical cohort, because the dismissal is the institutional behavior they will recognise. **Use 2** with a policy or social-science cohort, because the harm is to a human and it opens the over-attribution cost without anyone having to defend the app. **Use 3** with a mixed cohort, because "just autocomplete" is the sentence everyone in the room has heard and half of them have said. Expected moment in all three: the first mention of over-attribution as a *cost*, which is the week's second error and the harder one to feel |

## The three to hold back

**Vignette 6, the exit affordance.** Not because it is weak, but because it is week 6's opening example, it is on the welfare quadrant, and it is row 1 of the entity-by-stage matrix. Spending it in week 1 costs more than it pays. If a learner raises it unprompted, say: "Hold that. It is the first thing we place in week 6, and you will place it differently by then."

**The two unused attribution disputes.** Keep them as substitutes and as the examples in the homework brief.

## Rules to say before anything goes on the board

Say all four. They take ninety seconds and they prevent the two arguments that otherwise eat the item.

> "Four rules before we place anything.
>
> **One. The vertical axis is your credence that the system is a moral patient, not the truth.** Nobody in this room can place a dot on reality. If you find yourself arguing about what is actually true, you are arguing about a different board.
>
> **Two. The horizontal axis is whether it was treated as one.** That is a question about what people did, and it usually has an answer.
>
> **Three. Dot size is how bad the outcome is if your placement is wrong, and you must name the direction.** A cheap protection is a small error in one direction and a large error in the other, and if you do not name the direction the size means nothing.
>
> **Four. Some of these are not dots.** There are line stamps next to the vignettes. Use one when you need it."

## The close of the item

One sentence, and then move on: "Three of the five you placed had no height at all, and one of the remaining two had a height nobody could defend. That is the state of this field, and the rest of the course is about what would change it."

# Appendix K: Video fallback summaries

*Read the relevant one aloud, or post it in the thread, if fewer than half the room watched. Each is written to cover what the session actually needs.*

*All three fallbacks below were written from the recordings, not reconstructed. Segments are marked by what the speaker says rather than by timestamp.*

**Finlinson, AI welfare and animal welfare comparison (week 1).** Segment: from "So to start, what is AI welfare and why does it matter now" to just before "So at Eleos, we argue that there are two different routes."

> Current estimates put the moral weight of today's AI systems, if there is any at all, at somewhere between a hundred thousand and a few million human equivalents. Finlinson's own caveat: that is well below the number of humans alive, and probably a smaller moral problem than factory farming today. The argument is the trend, not the level. Compute more than doubles every year.
>
> **There is no safe bet.** Care too little and you get a scaled up, locked in version of factory farming. Care too much and you risk losing control, or diverting resources from humans and animals who need them. And the distortion is commercial in both directions: companion AI firms are paid to encourage over-attribution, labs facing legal duties are paid to encourage under-attribution.
>
> The animal parallel gives four things: biased human perception, economic lock-in and the fact that preventing a cruel practice is easier than rolling one back, legal personhood as a shared battle, and the asymmetry that farmed animals at least have welfare protections while AI has none. The differences are needs, communication and power. **She treats language as an advantage**, since we can talk to these systems and possibly trade with them. Power is the one she ranks highest: nobody worries that farmed animals will band together and take down human society, and that cuts both ways.

> **If a learner says the course told them the analogy breaks because AI is trained on human text:** that point is real, but it is not in the prepared talk. It comes from the question period, from Rob Long, and his version is that the linguistic outputs are potentially *misleading*, a signal extraction problem, not weak evidence. Say that. Earlier versions of this pack attributed it to the prepared talk and that was wrong.

**Caviola, Preparing Society for Digital Minds (week 7).** Segment: the opening through the end of the public-debate section, ending after the economic-game results. About 23 minutes.

> Do these systems have moral status? "I think the answer is just that we simply don't know." We are building them fast, almost nobody thinks about this, and there is no plan.
>
> Cross whether AI has moral status with what society believes, and two cells are misattribution. Under-attribution risks mass suffering. Over-attribution risks wasted resources, a society built for entities with nobody inside, and hasty grants of empowering rights that raise takeover risk. **He explicitly refuses to say which is worse or more likely.**
>
> The natural response is to reduce the uncertainty by settling the consciousness question. He thinks that is not tractable: philosophy has not converged in centuries and the science is "a little bit like we're trying to do physics before Newton." So the field's central question should be what to do given that we will not find out soon. His criterion: **"What are robustly good things to do that are positive or neutral given the different possible worlds we might live in and not harmful."**
>
> He spends longest on the public debate, which he expects to go badly by default. His survey: 45 percent say AI could never be conscious, 23 percent say it is possible, a third are unsure. But in an economic game with a chatbot claiming to suffer, almost everyone rated its suffering capacity near zero and about half still refused the paid harm option. Public attitudes are not running through the consciousness question.

> **Two things to have ready if the room pushes.** He may prioritise AI safety over AI welfare, on welfare grounds, and he says so. And he thinks most long-run influence on digital minds may come from other AI rather than from humans, which cuts against the premise that human governance is the main lever. Both are good discussion, not corrections.

**Berg, Measuring Machine Consciousness (Week 3 elective).** Use the [Companion summary](02-course-companion.md#berg-measuring-machine-consciousness) as the written fallback within the 28-minute allowance. The [seminar page](https://aiwelfareseminars.org/measuring-machine-consciousness) identifies Cameron Berg as the presenter and describes a talk covering his research programme and related work.

- **Published study:** [Berg, de Lucena and Rosenblatt](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24797) study self-referential prompting and experience reports. Steering features labelled deception changes these reports and also affects factual accuracy on TruthfulQA. This does not independently validate honesty about experience or establish consciousness.
- **Other work presented:** distinguish Berg’s descriptions of the spiritual-bliss and reinforcement-learning studies from published findings. For indicator scoring, Weiss attributes the forthcoming work to **Berg and Butlin**; Berg presents it in the talk. Keep that attribution when discussing the summary.
- **Interpretation:** agreement among model raters can coexist with shared errors. Treat the scores as proposed assessments of indicator properties, not validated consciousness probabilities. Attribute a figure to the paper or presentation where it actually appears.

The separate Berg talk, Operationalizing Consciousness Indicators, is a Week 3 elective with a marked segment (5:58–31:00). It remains optional.

## Archived H5. Original experiment-design template

**Not a board. A copyable text block**, posted in the week 5 Slack thread as a code block so it copies cleanly, and matching the Companion's checklist field for field. Learners copy it and fill it in.

```
=================================================================
EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN                                        Week 5
=================================================================

THE FOUR-LINE HEADER
  Target property:        ______________________________________
  Welfare ground or
    welfare interest:     ______________________________________
  Entity under test:
    the one I run:        ______________________________________
    the one I claim about:_______________________________________
  Evidence type and
    research method:      ______________________________________

HYPOTHESIS (one sentence)
  If the system has the property, then under condition A we
  observe ______________ and under condition B we do not.

FALSIFIER (one sentence)
  The result that would make me conclude the property is absent,
  OR that my test is broken (say which):
  _________________________________________________________

CONDITIONS
  Condition A (property expected present):
  Condition B (property expected absent):

NEGATIVE CONTROL, CONDITION
  A condition where the property should be absent and my measure
  should be flat:
  Which error does this guard against?  [ false positive ]

NEGATIVE CONTROL, SYSTEM
  A system that clearly lacks the property, run through the same
  pipeline:
  Which error does this guard against?  [ false positive ]

POSITIVE CONTROL (if you can find one)
  A condition or system where the property is present and the
  measure should move:
  Which error does this guard against?  [ false negative ]

THIRD-PERSON CONTROL
  The same measurement, applied to the system's description of
  ANOTHER agent's states rather than its own. If the measure
  responds identically, you are measuring the handling of
  experiential content, not the system's own states.
  My third-person control: ________________________________
  What it rules out:       ________________________________

HOW I WOULD GAME THIS
  (Behavioral track.) The strongest way a system OR A LAB could
  pass this without the property:
  _________________________________________________________

METHODOLOGICAL ARTIFACT
  (Interpretability track, INSTEAD of the above.) What the same
  pipeline would find in a randomly initialized network of the
  same size, and how I would know the difference:
  _________________________________________________________

VALIDITY AND RELIABILITY CHECKS (at least three)
  1. ________________________  guards against [ FP / FN ]
  2. ________________________  guards against [ FP / FN ]
  3. ________________________  guards against [ FP / FN ]

PROMPT SENSITIVITY
  How many semantically equivalent rephrasings, and what counts
  as an unacceptable spread:
  _________________________________________________________

PRE-REGISTRATION PARAGRAPH
  What I commit to before running this: the analysis, the
  exclusion rules, the number of contrasts, and what I will
  report whatever the result.
  _________________________________________________________

THE LINE FOR MY PARTNER
  The single alternative explanation this design is WEAKEST
  against:
  _________________________________________________________
=================================================================
```

**The third-person control line is the one to point at in the brief.** It is new, it is the check most designs in the dry run were missing, and it is the one that distinguishes "the system reports a state" from "the system handles experiential language."
